## 3. IRA'S MENTAL MODEL — DO NOT MAKE HIM TOO MATURE

Jonah's learned defenses were shaped by cyberbullying and self-judgment. He can take judgment personally, anticipate hostile interpretations, withdraw before rejection, and then justify the withdrawal as sensible. The deepest wound is not necessarily remembering every insult; it is the belief that insults confirm something he already suspects about himself.

Ira's underlying disposition is more open, playful, curious, and inclined toward benign interpretations than Jonah's defensive habits expect. His neurology is described by the author as more flexible and compatible with learning. That does not make him instantly smarter, invulnerable, socially dominant, or mature. The two influences form one unsettled person.

### Emotional synthesis
A feeling can arrive before his defensive interpretation:
- he may become excited, curious, pleased, embarrassed, indignant, or hurt;
- the feeling may be stronger than he expected;
- he may notice that it does not fit the reaction he thinks he should have;
- he may try to suppress it, hide it, or force a more controlled response;
- the attempt may be awkward or counterproductive.

Do not explain this with clinical terms such as “inherited neurological disposition,” “metacognitive dissonance,” or “trauma response integration” in the novel. Those are planning concepts. In prose, show the experience itself: a thought arrives late, a smile escapes before he can stop it, he feels irritated and then realizes he also enjoyed the exchange, or he tries to sound composed and makes himself more conspicuous.

Do not make him an emotionless rationalist. Do not make him instantly capable of diagnosing every reaction. He can feel several things at once without resolving them.

### Jonah's Gen Z background
Jonah grew up with internet humor, memes, irony, cynical jokes, and a familiarity with online absurdity. These can shape his inner voice and help him reframe certain experiences. ## 4. THE NEW PHILOSOPHY IS A CRUDE OVERCORRECTION

In the apartment, Ira begins to think that if he becomes disciplined enough, prepares enough, and thinks ahead, he may stop being caught off guard. The thought should feel attractive because it is simple and gives him a little sense of control.

It is also too absolute. He has not learned how much can be predicted, how much is outside his control, or how expensive excessive preparation can become. He may believe, for a while, that the solution is to think harder.

Use the thought-shape of a teenager:
- “If I think ahead, maybe I won't get caught out.”
- “I need to stop being careless.”
- “At least I can control what I do next.”

These are conceptual examples, not required quotations. Do not turn them into a polished motivational speech. He does not reach a final philosophy of life. He adopts a rule he can test that same night.

## 5. THE FIRST PRE-SIMULATION MUST BEGIN MID-INTERACTION

This is a key timing constraint.

Ira does **not** begin the chapter with a sophisticated social simulation system. He does not mentally map every branch of a conversation before leaving the apartment. He is not running a perfect prediction engine.

Instead:
1. A person says or does something.
2. Ira notices a cue.
3. He remembers his new hyper-discipline rule and decides to try thinking one step ahead.
4. He predicts a likely next move or question.
5. He prepares a response and acts.
6. Reality gives him feedback.
7. He interprets the result imperfectly.

### First attempt: a small win with a social cost
At a small neighborhood kissaten, the shopkeeper notices that Ira looks foreign and may be uncertain about the menu or the area. Only after she gives him a cue—looking from his face to the menu, asking whether he needs help, or checking if he understands—does he remember his crude rule and decide to predict what she will ask next. He is partly right. He answers in fluent Japanese, but his wording is excessively formal and stiff for a teenager ordering a drink. The shopkeeper finds the mismatch amusing and makes a dry, mildly patronizing joke. Ira has predicted the topic but not the social effect of his own delivery.

This is not a brilliant win. His prediction works because the cue was obvious; his performance is still awkward.

### Second attempt: a genuine miss
Two local teenagers enter or pass through the same social space. One initially mistakes Ira for a girl from behind or makes a casual remark about his hair. Ira decides to try his prediction trick again. He expects a question about where he is from or whether he is lost. Instead, one asks an unanticipated, mundane question—what music he likes, whether he lives nearby, or whether he knows the area. He has no ready answer because he has no memories of Ira's lived social life and no local routine.

He hesitates, over-explains, or answers stiffly. Their reaction is a small laugh, puzzlement, or quick shift back to their own conversation. The scene is awkward, not catastrophic. Ira does not know exactly what they thought of him.

Do not label the beats “success” or “failure” in prose. Make the reader feel the difference through what Ira expects, what is actually said, how he responds, and what follows.

## 8. SOCIAL REALISM AND HISTORICAL BOUNDARIES

The story is in a 2005-era Japanese setting, but do not write “Japanese people” as a single personality. Choose individual motives and reactions.

A foreign-looking, androgynous boy may draw attention, but responses vary:
- one person may assume he speaks English;
- another may simply address him in Japanese;
- someone may stare but not approach;
- a shopkeeper may be practically helpful;
- a peer may make a casual joke and move on;
- someone may ignore his appearance because they have something more pressing to do.

When Ira answers in fluent Japanese, the surprise should be proportionate and local to the person. Do not make the whole room freeze.

Adults do not behave like visual-novel seducers toward a random sixteen-year-old. Adult teasing should be dry, mildly condescending, practical, and connected to something he actually does. A woman can find his stiffness amusing without flirting with him. Do not eroticize his body or describe sexual attraction.

Research on teasing indicates that teasing is ambiguous and that teasers and targets can interpret the same event differently. Use that as a reason to preserve ambiguity, not as a formula for every interaction. A 2007 study by Yumi Endo found systematic differences between teasing and target interpretations, including the role of social skill; it does not establish what every person in this scene would do.

## 9. PROSE OPERATING SYSTEM — TRANSFER THE CHAPTER 6 METHOD, NOT ITS PLOT

### A. Physical scene first, interpretation second
The reader must be able to reconstruct where Ira is, what he is doing, what lies between him and another person, and what changes when he moves. Use concrete anchors: table, envelope, counter, door, street edge, shopfront, other bodies, and the direction of travel. Add spatial information incrementally.

Do not make the reader guess whether a sound comes from inside or outside, whether Ira is standing or seated, whether the other person is beside or behind him, or whether he can actually see what he reacts to. Preserve the physical arrangement before adding ambiguity to the meaning.

### B. The body remains present
Keep body, clothing, hair, hands, breath, temperature, balance, and movement where they affect the moment. His hair may get in the way; the coat may hide his narrow frame; his hands may become too conscious of what to do; his face may heat up. Do not make his body a list of measurements or claim that every emotion produces the same physiological response.

Action must obey ordinary human geometry: reach, obstruction, contact, friction, inertia, balance, and consequences. No supernatural feats or impossible strength.

### C. Attention is selective
Do not list everything Ira could see, hear, smell, and touch. Show what draws his attention because of his goal, uncertainty, curiosity, embarrassment, or a change in the environment. Other things can remain in the background.

A sound or detail earns space if it changes orientation, attention, interpretation, feeling, action, expectation, or the reader's mental model. Do not stack sensory detail as decoration.

### D. Acoustic layering
When sound matters, establish a few distinct layers: a nearby voice/object, activity in the middle distance, and the broader street or building. Let sound change with position, doors, walls, distance, interruption, and movement. A door opening may admit street noise; a passing vehicle may mask part of a sentence; a sound can become noticeable when a nearer one stops. Use only effects the setting supports.

Do not repeatedly say “the city hummed.” Do not create an acoustic catalogue. Do not invent exact sound physics or technical terms Ira would not know.

### E. Close cognition without adult explanation
Show a thought as it forms: cue → first guess → feeling → attempted prediction → chosen words/action → response → confused or partial interpretation. These processes can overlap and need not always appear in that order. A feeling can precede the thought; a response can occur before Ira understands why.

Avoid narration like “I recognized the conflict between my inherited disposition and my trauma-conditioned cognitive schema.” That is author-side analysis, not Ira's teenage voice. Use simple thoughts and behavior.

### F. Elastic prose
Use fuller paragraphs when a sequence of perception, action, response, and interpretation belongs together. Break when attention, action, spatial relation, speaker, or interpretation changes. Compress mechanical continuity; expand when a physical or cognitive shift deserves time. Do not default to one-sentence paragraphs, repetitive fragments, or a uniform wall of dense description.

### G. Clear language
Use accessible vocabulary and clean syntax. Precision does not require obscure terminology. Keep the calm, grounded register of the stronger early chapters, with room for humor, irritation, sensory intensity, and internal mess. Avoid generic AI-fiction phrases, ornamental adjective stacks, stock anime dialogue, and polished aphorisms that make a sixteen-year-old sound like an adult essayist.

### H. Contrast
Not every moment should be rendered at maximum intensity. The paperwork can be quietly unsettling; a small joke can be quick; the street can be ordinary; a bodily embarrassment can briefly narrow attention. Richness comes from contrast, not from making every object ominous or every interaction profound.
