# Chapter Six — Somewhere to Go

I woke up before the alarm.

Again.

For a few seconds I stared at the ceiling without moving. The room was quiet except for the faint sounds coming through the walls, distant enough that I couldn't make out any words. A thin band of morning light had found its way through the curtains and stretched across the floor, stopping just short of the bed.

Then I remembered where I was.

The apartment looked different from yesterday, although nothing had actually changed. I knew where the bathroom was now. I knew which drawer held the wallet, where I'd left the school papers, and which cupboard contained the few things I'd found that could pass for breakfast. It was a surprisingly useful improvement.

I reached for my phone.

6:41.

My alarm was set for seven.

For a moment I considered going back to sleep. Then I remembered yesterday's journey, the map folded on the desk, and the number of times I'd managed to question my sense of direction.

No.

Absolutely not.

I sat up. My hair had spread across the pillow and down my back during the night. I gathered it over one shoulder, already annoyed by how much of it there was.

“Morning.”

My voice still sounded strange.

Less strange than yesterday, though.

Maybe that was the problem.

I was getting used to it.

I dressed using the clothes I'd found the day before. Whoever Ira had been, he apparently had a better system for keeping clothes organised than I did. That wasn't difficult.

The examination papers were still on the desk. I checked the wallet, money, identification, phone and application documents, then checked them again because losing any of them would have been a particularly stupid way to ruin the only plan I had.

The map came last.

I unfolded it, followed the route with my finger, folded it again, then unfolded it once more to make sure I hadn't somehow misunderstood it overnight.

Station.

Transfer.

Kuoh.

Academy.

Yesterday I had managed to find the place without getting completely lost. That counted for something.

Today I intended to repeat the achievement.

---

There wasn't much food in the kitchen. I found bread and eggs, along with something in the refrigerator I wasn't entirely confident identifying and therefore had no intention of eating.

The eggs were safer.

While they cooked, I stood beside the counter and watched the neighbourhood through the window. Morning had already started gathering below. A delivery van stopped at the curb while someone carried boxes toward a shop. In the building opposite, a window opened and a piece of fabric was shaken out over the street. A bicycle passed beneath me, its tires making a soft hiss against the road, and somewhere farther away a truck shifted gears.

The smell of cooking drifted up from another apartment.

For a few seconds I just stood there.

Yesterday, all of this had been evidence that I was somewhere I didn't belong. This morning it was simply morning.

The difference was small.

It mattered anyway.

I ate quickly, washed the plate and checked the time.

7:18.

“Okay.”

I picked up my bag and went back into the bedroom.

The apartment was still strange. The furniture belonged to someone else. The books belonged to someone else. The life contained in the drawers belonged to someone else.

But I'd slept here twice.

I'd eaten here.

I'd showered here.

I'd stood in this room and cried until I couldn't anymore.

Maybe that was how places became yours. Not because you owned them, but because things happened there.

I locked the door behind me.

---

The street was easier to look at this morning.

Yesterday I'd been too busy figuring out where I was to notice much beyond the route itself. Now I could afford to pay attention to the buildings along the road: concrete and plaster walls, narrow windows, balconies with railings at different heights, air-conditioning units fitted wherever someone had found enough wall space, and pipes running down the sides of several buildings.

Utility wires crossed overhead in loose black lines.

The pavement wasn't perfectly even. Patches of yesterday's moisture had dried more slowly beneath the shade cast by the buildings, leaving darker shapes along the edges of the road. A shopkeeper wrestled with a metal shutter while the smell of frying food drifted around the corner. Two schoolchildren passed me talking quickly enough that I couldn't catch what they were saying.

I kept walking.

The station was farther than it looked on the map.

Of course it was.

By the time I reached the main road, I was checking the route again. Cars passed in both directions, their tires whispering over the damp sections of pavement. A bus pulled away from a stop and left behind a brief cloud of exhaust that thinned almost immediately in the morning air.

The traffic light changed.

People started crossing.

I crossed with them.

Five minutes later I saw the pharmacy.

Then the station entrance.

I smiled.

“Didn't get lost.”

That felt like an achievement.

---

The station was already busy in the particular way places became busy when large numbers of people all needed to be somewhere at roughly the same time. Shoes clicked against the floor. Conversations overlapped beneath the announcements coming through the speakers, and every few seconds someone moved past quickly enough to catch the edge of my attention.

I stopped beneath the departure board and checked the platform, then the destination, then the time.

For once, the characters matched the information I'd written down.

Everything made sense.

I bought a ticket and went downstairs.

The platform smelled faintly of metal, dust and whatever someone had eaten recently. A vending machine stood beside one of the pillars, so I bought a bottle of water and, after looking at the remaining money in my wallet, a small packet of something that looked filling enough to count as breakfast.

I opened it while I waited.

It was sweet.

Very sweet.

I stared at the packet.

“Why is everything here trying to kill me with sugar?”

Nobody answered.

A man standing nearby glanced at me, then at the snack, and decided whatever was happening wasn't his problem.

I ate another piece.

It wasn't bad.

The train arrived with a rush of air along the platform. People shifted toward the marked areas before the doors opened, and when they did, everyone moved with the quiet efficiency of people who had done the same thing hundreds of times.

I followed.

Inside, there were already plenty of passengers. I found a place near the window and held onto the overhead strap.

The doors closed.

The train moved.

---

For the first part of the journey, the city stayed dense. Rows of houses pressed against apartment blocks, small shops filled the gaps between larger buildings, and concrete walls ran beside parking lots and narrow roads. Utility poles passed at regular intervals, carrying wires away into the distance.

Then, gradually, the arrangement loosened.

Trees appeared between buildings. Some roads widened. There were stretches where I could see farther than the next intersection, and the spaces between houses began to matter almost as much as the houses themselves.

People came and went at every stop.

An old man carrying a folded newspaper.

A woman with a sleeping child against her shoulder.

Three students talking over one another.

A businessman who spent almost the entire journey staring at his phone.

A little boy who kept pressing his face against the window until his mother pulled him back.

None of them knew anything about me.

That was strangely comforting.

Nobody expected me to explain where I had come from. Nobody knew that the person standing beside them had died. Nobody knew that the body I was wearing belonged to someone named Ira Lowe.

To them, I was just another passenger.

I looked at my reflection in the window.

Long hair.

Pale face.

Green eyes.

I looked away.

Not today.

I wasn't going to spend the whole trip thinking about myself.

Outside, the scenery continued to change. A cluster of factories gave way to a broad road, then houses with small gardens, then another dense stretch of buildings. The farther we went, the more I started recognising the rhythm rather than the individual places.

Dense.

Open.

Commercial.

Residential.

Green.

Dense again.

Yesterday I had experienced all of it as one huge unfamiliar place. Today I could see that it had layers.

That made it easier to understand.

At the next stop, I got off and checked the sign against the map.

Correct.

The transfer platform was on the opposite side of the station. I followed the signs, found a bench and sat down.

A woman beside me was eating from a paper bag.

The smell made me hungry again.

I checked the time.

8:04.

I had time.

I bought another snack from a machine. This one wasn't nearly as sweet.

I considered that a victory.

The next train arrived.

I boarded.

---

The second train was less crowded, and this time I managed to get a seat by the window.

The city outside looked different now. There were more trees and low buildings, and the roads seemed wider. Houses had more space between them. It wasn't rural—not even close—but there was room.

Room between things.

I liked that.

I didn't know why.

Maybe because the first few days of my new life had felt like everything was pressing in at once: a body I didn't recognise, a name I didn't know, a world I hadn't asked for, and a future I couldn't see.

Out here, there was space.

The train slowed.

I checked the station name.

Kuoh.

I stood before the doors opened.

My heart beat a little faster.

Not from fear.

Something else.

Anticipation, maybe.

I stepped off the train.

---

Kuoh was already awake.

The station opened onto a broad street lined with shops. A bakery had fogged windows from the heat inside, and the smell of bread escaped whenever the door opened. A bicycle rack stood outside a convenience store. Students moved along the pavement in groups while a delivery driver carried boxes into a restaurant.

Across the street, a woman stood outside a florist trimming stems and dropping the cut pieces into a bucket.

The air smelled different from the neighbourhood around my apartment. There was less exhaust and more food, with something green underneath it—trees, wet leaves, grass.

I unfolded the map.

The academy wasn't far.

I started walking.

The route looked simple.

Which should have made me suspicious.

Ten minutes later, I reached an intersection that wasn't on the map in the way I expected. The road split in two, and I stopped between them, checking the street name against the map and then checking the map again.

I knew roughly where I was.

That was the important part.

Yesterday I would have panicked.

Today I looked around.

A bus stop stood across the road. Beneath its timetable was a local map showing the surrounding streets in much more detail than mine did.

I walked over, found the academy, then found my position.

I'd taken the wrong branch.

Not by much.

I laughed quietly.

“Okay.”

I folded my map.

“I can work with that.”

I turned around.

This time I knew exactly where I was going.

---

The closer I got to the academy, the more students I saw. Groups formed naturally along the sidewalks. Some walked quickly, already discussing the examination. Others took their time. A few looked as though they would rather have been anywhere else.

One boy had his head buried in a book while walking and nearly collided with a utility pole. His friend caught him by the shoulder.

“You almost died.”

“I was reading.”

“That doesn't help.”

They continued walking.

A bicycle went past.

Then another.

A woman stepped out of a shop carrying a paper cup and nearly bumped into a student.

“Sorry.”

“No, I'm sorry.”

They moved apart.

Normal.

All of it was normal.

I found that I liked it.

There was no great revelation in it. No dramatic feeling. Just the quiet relief of seeing people continue to be people.

The academy appeared beyond the trees.

I slowed.

Yesterday I had only seen it from a distance. Today I was actually walking toward it.

Stone walls and fencing marked parts of the perimeter, broken by entrances and pedestrian paths. Trees lined sections of the grounds, their leaves shifting whenever the wind passed through. Beyond them, the main building rose above the property, pale walls catching the morning light while darker sections of the roof disappeared behind the upper branches.

Students moved through the gates.

Teachers stood near the entrance.

A few parents waited along the side of the road.

Someone from the administration was checking papers at a folding table.

A sign directed examination applicants toward another entrance.

I followed it.

There were already dozens of people waiting.

I joined the line.

---

Registration was painfully ordinary.

Name.

Identification.

Application number.

Document.

Signature.

Wait.

Move forward.

Repeat.

The staff member checking my papers barely looked at me.

“Applicant number?”

I handed over the form.

He checked it.

“Ira Lowe?”

“Yes.”

“Entrance examination?”

“Yes.”

“Please wait in the designated area after registration.”

He stamped something.

“Next.”

That was it.

I walked away.

For some reason, I almost laughed.

After everything that had happened, my new existence had apparently reached the point where somebody could look at me, confirm my paperwork, and tell me to stand in a line.

There was something reassuring about that.

I found the waiting area.

Applicants were scattered across benches and along the edge of the courtyard. Some studied. Some talked. Some stared at their phones. One girl sat with her eyes closed, quietly mouthing formulas to herself, while two boys compared notes nearby.

A parent spoke with a staff member near the doors.

Somewhere behind the main building, a lawn mower started. The engine rattled for a few seconds before settling into a steady mechanical hum.

I sat down.

The sun had climbed higher. Shadows beneath the trees had shortened, and a breeze moved across the courtyard hard enough to lift the ends of my hair.

I caught them before they blew into my face.

“Annoying.”

A voice nearby laughed.

I looked over.

A girl stood a few metres away in the academy uniform, dark hair falling neatly around her face and glasses catching a brief reflection of sunlight. She carried several documents against her chest while speaking quietly with another student.

I didn't know her.

There was nothing particularly strange about her. She simply looked like someone who belonged here.

The other student said something. She adjusted her glasses and answered, then noticed me looking.

Our eyes met for a second.

I looked away.

Not because I was embarrassed.

Mostly because I didn't want to stare.

When I looked back, she had already turned away. She continued talking for another moment before walking toward the administrative building.

That was all.

I didn't think much of it.

The courtyard settled back into its strange kind of waiting.

People weren't doing nothing. They were all doing small things—checking watches, reading, talking, drinking water, adjusting bags, looking toward the doors. It was the kind of activity that happened whenever a large group of people knew something was about to begin.

I checked my phone.

8:47.

Twelve minutes.

I leaned back.

The academy was quieter than the streets outside, but it wasn't silent. Footsteps crossed stone. Voices carried from the far side of the courtyard. A door opened somewhere inside the building, followed by the muffled sound of someone speaking.

The lawn mower stopped.

A bird landed briefly on the edge of a roof before flying away.

I looked up.

The sky was pale blue through the branches. Sunlight reached the upper windows while the lower ones remained in shade, and the glass reflected pieces of the trees so that the building seemed to disappear and reappear whenever the leaves moved.

For some reason, I found myself thinking about the apartment.

The desk.

The kitchen.

The lock on the door.

That place still wasn't mine.

Not really.

But it was somewhere I could return to.

That was enough.

Yesterday I had been trying to understand how I had ended up here. Today I was sitting in a school courtyard waiting to take an entrance examination.

It was ridiculous.

But it was also simple.

I had a place to sleep.

I had money.

I had a name.

I had somewhere to go tomorrow.

Maybe that was what a second life was supposed to feel like at first.

Not grand.

Not meaningful.

Just possible.

A bell rang somewhere inside the academy.

The courtyard changed almost immediately.

Students stopped talking. Staff began moving toward the entrance. Applicants stood, chairs scraped against the ground, bags were lifted from the pavement, and people checked their papers one last time.

I stood with them.

Another bell sounded.

Louder this time.

A staff member opened the doors.

“Entrance examination applicants, please proceed inside.”

I tightened my grip on the strap of my bag.

For a second, I stood still.

Then I moved with everyone else.
