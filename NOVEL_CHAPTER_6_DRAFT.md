# Chapter 6

I woke before the alarm and spent several seconds staring at the ceiling without remembering why.

Morning light had already found its way around the curtains. It lay in pale bands across the wall and the edge of the bed, bright enough to pick out the small imperfections in the paint that I had never noticed the day before. Outside, something heavy rolled along the street, paused, then continued with a low mechanical rattle. A bicycle bell sounded farther away.

Then I remembered.

The examination.

Today.

I reached toward the alarm clock, saw the time, and immediately regretted being conscious.

6:17.

"That's disgusting."

I rolled onto my back and pulled the blanket over my face.

For thirty seconds, I considered going back to sleep.

For another thirty seconds, I seriously considered it.

Then I remembered that I had already spent enough of my new life discovering that plans became significantly worse when I ignored them.

I sat up.

The room was cold enough that I noticed it immediately, though not unpleasantly so. My feet found the floor. Green hair slipped forward over my shoulder when I bent to reach for my clothes, and I pushed it back without thinking.

I stopped halfway through the movement.

That was new.

Not the hair. The fact that I hadn't thought about it first.

A week ago, yesterday, whatever the correct measurement of my existence was now, I would have been staring at myself every five seconds.

This morning I was annoyed because it was in the way.

I wasn't sure whether that was progress or just fatigue.

Probably both.

The clothes I had laid out were where I'd left them. I dressed more quickly than I expected, straightening the collar and checking the sleeves before moving on to the next thing. The motions were becoming less deliberate. I didn't have to consciously remember where everything went.

That was another small thing I wasn't going to make a big deal out of.

I went into the kitchen.

The apartment was quiet in the way places were quiet before everyone else had started their day. The refrigerator hummed softly. Somewhere in the building, plumbing clicked behind a wall. Morning traffic had begun outside, but the windows softened it into a distant wash.

I opened the cupboard and found enough for breakfast.

Nothing remarkable. Something to eat, something warm to drink, a plate, a cup.

I stood there for a moment with the cupboard door open.

There was a strange intimacy to knowing where something was when I couldn't remember learning it.

My hand had already reached for the cup before I consciously decided which one I wanted.

I looked down at it.

"...Okay."

I shut the cupboard.

Breakfast was simple, but I made myself eat properly. I had no idea how long the trip would take, and the last thing I needed was to discover halfway through it that apparently being sixteen in a new body came with a completely different relationship with hunger.

That thought made me pause.

Sixteen.

I still wasn't used to that either.

My mind had spent years attaching seventeen to me so firmly that the number had become part of my internal furniture. The body was sixteen. I was seventeen. Somehow both statements remained true.

I finished eating before the thought could become complicated.

The bag was waiting by the table.

I checked it once.

Documents.

Money.

Pens.

School materials.

The route.

I checked everything again.

Then I looked at my own hands.

"That's enough."

I checked the route a third time anyway.

There was no glowing map on a phone to rescue me if I made a mistake. I had a folded piece of paper with station names and connections written clearly enough to follow if I actually paid attention.

That was the important part.

Actually paying attention.

I folded it again and put it somewhere I could reach without unpacking half the bag.

Before leaving, I walked through the apartment once more.

Not because I'd forgotten anything.

Because I wanted to.

The bed was made badly but acceptably. The cup from breakfast was in the sink. The clothes I had not chosen were still where I'd left them. The mirror caught a piece of morning light from the window and threw it across the opposite wall.

I stood by the door with my hand on the lock.

Yesterday, this place had felt like somewhere I was occupying.

This morning, I knew where the cups were.

I knew which drawer stuck.

I knew which floorboard complained if I put my weight on it the wrong way.

I knew where I had left my bag.

That wasn't home.

Not yet.

But it was beginning to feel like a place where I lived.

I locked the door.

The hallway smelled faintly of detergent and cooked food. A door opened farther down just as I reached the stairs, and an older woman stepped out carrying a small bag. We exchanged a quick greeting. I followed the flow of her movement toward the entrance, then stepped outside.

The rain had gone, but the street still remembered it.

Water sat in shallow lines along the edges of the pavement. Tires whispered through damp patches. The concrete walls of the surrounding buildings were darker near the ground, and little beads of water still clung to metal railings where the sun hadn't reached them.

The morning was much brighter than the previous night had suggested.

That changed everything.

The same buildings that had looked anonymous in darkness now had colour: faded paint, cream plaster, dull metal shutters, brown wooden frames, patches of moss where moisture had collected. Utility poles rose between them with a tangle of overhead wires running from one building to another and disappearing along the street.

A shop owner was lifting a shutter.

The metal rattled upward, exposing shelves behind the glass.

A bicycle leaned against the wall beside him.

A delivery truck was stopped farther ahead, and the driver was carrying boxes through a narrow doorway while a woman stood inside directing him with one hand.

A child in a yellow raincoat complained to his mother about something I couldn't hear.

The mother did not look impressed.

I kept walking.

It was strange how quickly the world could become ordinary.

Yesterday I had been trying to understand what had happened to me. Today I was worried about catching a train.

Somewhere in between those two things, life had apparently decided to continue without asking permission.

I found myself smiling.

Then I realized I was smiling at nothing and stopped.

The station entrance appeared at the end of the street.

I slowed before going in.

I had used public transportation before. That did not mean I trusted myself with it.

Inside, the station had its own rhythm. Footsteps came and went across the tiled floor. Announcements arrived through ceiling speakers, blurred slightly by distance and echo. People moved with the efficiency of those who had made the same journey enough times that they no longer needed to think about every step.

I did.

I found the ticket machines and stood in front of them.

There were buttons.

There were numbers.

There were station names.

There was a person behind me who was definitely waiting for me to stop staring.

I stepped aside.

"Go ahead."

The man gave me a small nod and moved forward.

I unfolded the route again.

Once I had the fare, the machine became much less intimidating.

Coins.

Button.

Ticket.

Change.

I picked everything up and checked the paper twice before putting it away.

The gate accepted the ticket without complaint.

That felt like a personal victory.

I found the platform and waited.

The train wasn't there yet.

Good.

For once, being early had worked.

I stood beneath the shelter and watched the station wake around me.

A newspaper folded under someone's arm.

A businessman checking his watch.

Two students arguing quietly about something while sharing a drink.

An older woman carefully arranging the contents of a shopping bag.

A young man with wired headphones leaning against a pillar, eyes closed.

No one was looking at me.

Or, if they were, they were doing it subtly enough that I didn't notice.

That mattered.

I'd expected being in this body to make every public space uncomfortable.

Instead, after a while, it became background.

The train arrived with a rush of wind.

People shifted toward the marked areas before the doors opened. When they did, passengers stepped out first, then the waiting crowd moved in.

I followed.

The carriage was crowded without being packed. I found a place near the window and held the overhead strap.

The train moved.

For the first few stops, the view changed only gradually.

Rows of buildings slid past the window. Narrow roads appeared and disappeared between them. Utility wires crossed the view at different heights. Cars waited at intersections, their roofs bright under the morning sky. Laundry hung from balconies. Small gardens occupied whatever strips of ground the buildings hadn't taken.

Then the density began to loosen.

It wasn't dramatic enough to mark a single moment when the city changed.

One building disappeared.

A little more sky appeared.

Then a wider road.

Then trees.

The next station had fewer people waiting.

The next had more.

A group of schoolchildren got on, their voices filling the carriage for several minutes before they settled into quieter conversations.

An elderly man boarded carrying a newspaper and a small bag. He looked around, found an empty seat, and sat down with the practiced certainty of someone who knew exactly how long his journey would take.

I watched him unfold the paper.

That was the thing about travel I had never really appreciated before.

Distance wasn't just the space between two points.

It was everything that happened while you crossed it.

A person got on.

Someone else got off.

A building became a field.

A field became houses.

A stranger bought a drink.

Someone fell asleep.

A station passed.

Then another.

I rested my forehead lightly against the window.

For a moment, I remembered taking trains before.

Not this route.

Not this country.

Not this life.

Just the sensation of watching the outside world move while knowing I was going somewhere specific.

The memory was so ordinary that it hurt more than I expected.

I looked away from the glass.

I wasn't going to spend the morning mourning a train journey from a life I couldn't return to.

I had an exam.

That was enough.

At one of the larger stations, I got off to change trains.

The concourse was busier.

People crossed in several directions, and I followed the signs while trying not to look like someone who had no idea what he was doing.

I failed.

Probably.

Still, I found the next platform.

There was a small shop near the stairs. The smell reached me before I saw the food.

I stopped.

I checked the time.

Still early.

I had eaten breakfast.

I was not hungry.

My stomach disagreed.

"Traitor."

I bought something small and a drink.

The woman behind the counter handed them over, and I gave her the money.

For one second I hesitated, unsure whether I had counted correctly.

She waited.

I counted again.

Correct.

I took the food and moved aside.

The first bite was warm enough to make me realize how hungry I actually was.

I stood near the edge of the concourse and ate while people passed around me.

It wasn't special food.

That was exactly why I liked it.

No revelation came with it.

No mysterious memory.

No sudden connection to some hidden part of Ira.

It was just food.

Warm.

Filling.

Ordinary.

I finished it, folded the wrapper, and found a bin.

Then I checked the route again.

The next train was on the opposite side of the platform.

Of course it was.

I walked down the stairs, crossed beneath the tracks, and came back up on the other side.

By the time I reached the platform, the train was still several minutes away.

I sat.

My bag rested against my knees.

The station speakers announced the next arrival.

I listened carefully this time.

Not because I suddenly understood everything.

Because I had learned that listening carefully was cheaper than correcting a mistake later.

The train arrived.

I boarded.

The scenery changed again.

This part of the journey felt less crowded. The buildings were lower, and spaces between them stayed open longer. Houses appeared behind walls and hedges. Small commercial strips sat beside stretches of residential road. There were more trees, more patches of grass, more sky.

I watched the landscape without trying to name everything.

A narrow river appeared beside the tracks for several minutes.

Then it vanished behind buildings.

A line of laundry moved in the wind.

A football field appeared behind a fence.

A construction site exposed the raw grey skeleton of a building.

Then the train passed beneath a road bridge and the view changed again.

I checked the station name.

Then the route.

Then the station name.

Something was wrong.

I looked up at the board.

Wrong platform?

Wrong direction?

I unfolded the paper.

The answer came slowly.

I had understood the transfer correctly.

I had misunderstood which service I was supposed to take after it.

Not catastrophically.

Just enough to put me somewhere I didn't need to be.

I stared at the station board.

"You've got to be kidding me."

The train doors opened.

People got off.

I stayed where I was for another few seconds, thinking.

Yesterday, I might have panicked.

This time I looked back at the route.

There was another service from the same station.

The difference was printed directly beneath the destination.

I had simply not bothered to read it.

That was irritating.

It was also fixable.

I got off, found the correct platform, and waited.

A station employee noticed me looking at the board.

"Excuse me," I said.

He turned.

I showed him the paper.

"Is this the one for the next transfer?"

He checked it, then pointed toward the opposite platform.

I followed his explanation, thanked him, and moved.

No catastrophe.

No wandering around the city for an hour.

Just a mistake, a question, and a correction.

I filed that away.

I could do this.

The thought arrived quietly.

I could actually do this.

The next train carried me farther from the dense urban areas I'd seen earlier.

The spaces between buildings continued to widen.

The light felt different when there was less concrete around it. Sunlight reached the pavement in larger pieces. Trees cast moving shadows across walls and parked cars. Houses sat farther back from the road. Some had small gardens, some had narrow strips of planted ground, and others were separated from the street by simple walls or hedges.

The city hadn't disappeared.

It had simply stopped pressing so closely against itself.

Then I began seeing more students.

One at a crossing.

Then three.

Then a group near a shop.

Then another cluster farther down the road.

School bags.

Uniforms.

Bicycles.

The direction of movement became obvious.

I checked the paper one last time.

Kuoh.

The name was printed there.

I had already learned what it meant.

Not much.

A place.

A school.

A point on a route.

But now I was actually approaching it.

I got off and continued on foot.

The streets around the station were quieter than the areas I'd passed through earlier. A convenience-style shop sat near the corner. A bakery sign hung above another storefront. Cars moved through the intersection without the constant congestion of the larger urban stretches.

The pavement was still damp in places.

Water had gathered beneath a hedge and reflected a slice of blue sky whenever the leaves shifted.

A bicycle went past me.

Then another.

A woman opened a gate and shook water from a cloth.

Somewhere nearby, oil hissed in a kitchen.

The smell made me hungry again.

I ignored it.

I had already eaten.

The Academy appeared gradually.

First the students became more numerous.

Then the school bags.

Then the uniforms.

Then the gates.

I slowed.

The school did not look like some impossible monument.

It looked like a school.

That was strangely reassuring.

The entrance was busy enough to feel important without becoming chaotic. Staff members moved between the gate and the buildings beyond it. Applicants stood with parents or guardians. Students crossed the grounds in groups, some talking loudly, others apparently trying to avoid being late.

A sign near the entrance directed applicants toward registration.

I followed it.

Inside, the administrative process was exactly as exciting as administrative processes tended to be.

There was a line.

Then another line.

A desk.

A form.

A staff member checking documents.

A question about where I needed to go.

I answered.

Another direction.

I followed it.

At one point I stopped because three corridors appeared to lead toward roughly the same part of the building.

A student noticed me looking at the signs.

"Entrance examination?"

"Yeah."

She pointed.

"That way."

"Thanks."

"No problem."

She disappeared into the crowd before I could say anything else.

I followed the indicated corridor.

The school smelled faintly of floor cleaner, paper, fabric, and the warm air of a building that had been open since early morning.

Classroom doors stood open.

Some rooms were empty.

Others had adults arranging desks.

Applicants passed in groups.

Parents spoke quietly near the walls.

A boy in front of me kept checking the same document every thirty seconds.

A girl beside him was talking so quickly to her friend that I couldn't catch half of it.

Someone laughed near the stairwell.

Someone else was already complaining about the exam.

I almost felt normal.

That was the strange part.

Not happy.

Not completely settled.

Just normal.

I had a bag.

I had paperwork.

I was nervous about a test.

People around me had no reason to know that I had died.

Or that I had woken up in another body.

Or that I had spent the last few days trying to work out what my existence even meant.

To them, I was just another applicant.

There was something comforting about that.

I found the registration area and completed the remaining steps.

The staff member checked my documents, pointed me toward the waiting area, and gave me directions I repeated silently to make sure I remembered them.

I thanked her.

Then I moved away from the desk.

The campus opened up beyond the building.

From there I could see more of it.

Trees bordered part of the grounds. Sunlight moved through the leaves and broke across the pavement in shifting patches. Windows reflected pieces of sky. The morning breeze carried voices from one side of the campus to another, sometimes clear enough to understand a word, sometimes reduced to indistinct sound.

Students who already belonged here moved differently from the applicants.

They knew where they were going.

They didn't look at signs.

They didn't stop at corners.

They moved around one another without thinking.

I watched them for a moment.

That was going to take time.

I wasn't going to pretend otherwise.

Then I noticed a girl standing near one of the buildings.

Dark hair.

Glasses.

Her uniform was neat enough that it almost looked deliberate, every part of it sitting exactly where it should. She was speaking quietly with another student, but her attention seemed to move between the conversation and the people passing nearby.

Not suspiciously.

Just attentively.

Someone approached them and said something.

The girl answered.

Her voice carried only a few words before the surrounding noise swallowed the rest.

For a second, her eyes moved in my direction.

We looked at each other.

I looked away.

Not because anything had happened.

Because staring at strangers was still weird.

When I looked back, she was already walking toward another part of the building.

I shrugged.

No idea who she was.

Probably just another student.

The waiting area was beginning to fill.

I found a place where I could sit without blocking anyone and set my bag against my legs.

8:43.

I still had time.

That surprised me.

The journey had felt long.

Not in a bad way.

Long enough that I could remember individual pieces of it: the first station, the train window, the food, the wrong platform, the employee pointing me in the right direction, the widening streets, the school gate.

I knew how I had gotten here.

That mattered.

Yesterday, Kuoh had been information.

Today, it was pavement under my shoes.

The school was not a name on paper anymore.

It had hallways.

Windows.

Trees.

Voices.

A smell of floor cleaner.

People who were late.

People who were bored.

People who were nervous.

People who belonged here.

I leaned back slightly.

For the first time since waking up in that apartment, I wasn't trying to solve my entire existence.

I had a smaller problem.

Pass the exam.

Everything else could wait.

A staff member called for applicants to begin moving toward the examination rooms.

People stood.

Chairs scraped against the floor.

Conversations changed volume and then quieted as groups began forming.

I checked my documents one last time.

Still there.

Pens.

Still there.

Money.

Still there.

I closed the bag.

Outside, sunlight moved across the campus as the trees shifted in the breeze.

Someone called for a group to move forward.

I stood with the others.

My stomach tightened.

Not badly.

Just enough to remind me that I was nervous.

I adjusted the strap over my shoulder.

The clock near the corridor was approaching nine.

For a moment, I looked back toward the campus.

It was strange.

A few days ago, I had been Jonah.

Then I had become something I still didn't fully understand.

Now I was standing in a school I had never attended, carrying documents under a name that had only recently become mine, surrounded by people who knew nothing about the life I had left behind.

And somehow, the thought didn't feel terrifying.

It felt possible.

The bell rang.

The sound travelled through the building and across the grounds.

Conversation stopped in pockets.

Staff began directing applicants toward their rooms.

Everyone moved at once.

I moved with them.

At nine o'clock, the examination began.
