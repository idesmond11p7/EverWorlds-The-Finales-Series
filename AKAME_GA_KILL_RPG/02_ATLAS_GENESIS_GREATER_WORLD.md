# 02 — Atlas Genesis: The Greater World of Akame ga Kill! RPG

**Status:** Original worldbuilding proposal, deliberately expansive; not manga canon.  
**Design objective:** Build a full dark-fantasy planet whose political, ecological, cultural, technological and supernatural complexity vastly exceeds the narrow geographic focus of the original series, while preserving the manga's Empire incident as an internally coherent historical chain.

## 0. Separation of realities

There are three layers, not one blended soup:

1. **Manga Continuity Layer:** the Empire, its people, the Revolutionary Army, Night Raid, the Teigu/Shingu history, Danger Beasts, Wakoku and other source-established elements. These retain their manga identities and chronology.
2. **Greater World Layer:** new continents, nations, cities, peoples, species, religions, cults, magic traditions, industrial systems, historical eras, trade networks and conflicts. These are original additions designed around the known setting, never misrepresented as canon.
3. **Campaign Causality Layer:** changes caused by the player and by autonomous world events. This layer records exactly which events diverge from the source chronology and why.

The default is **coexistence without interference**. The greater world is real, but it does not automatically hijack the manga's Empire incident. Contact requires a causal route. A player can create one directly or indirectly, and consequences then propagate along plausible connections.

## 1. Planetary frame — provisional physical model

### Working name: Avarra

**Avarra is an original expansion name**, not a canon name established by the manga. If primary-source research later identifies a canonical planetary/world name, the naming decision must be revisited without erasing this design history.

Avarra is a single vast terrestrial world with a larger habitable surface and more extensive geographical variety than the setting impression conveyed by the original manga. To avoid pretending the source supplies planetary measurements, use a **provisional map-scale model** rather than asserting a canon radius. The first atlas pass will use a world-map surface roughly 2.4 times Earth's surface as a design target, subject to a gravity/atmosphere sanity check. This is an expansion parameter, not a manga fact. If the final model uses Earthlike gravity, the planetary mass/density and atmospheric behavior must be made internally consistent rather than assuming size has no physical effects.

The world contains:
- Several major continents, archipelagos, enclosed seas and open-ocean basins.
- A connected but difficult global ocean system, with some coastlines separated by dangerous currents, seasonal storms, reefs, ice belts or political blockades.
- Vast inland regions where overland journeys can take months.
- Multiple major mountain chains and plateaus, broad river basins, rain-shadow deserts, wetlands, temperate forests, boreal forests, tropical belts and high-altitude cold zones.
- Regions where Danger Beasts dominate the food web and routes are shaped by seasonal migration.
- Isolated environments with distinct life histories: abyssal seas, volcanic uplands, salt basins, glacial interiors, canopy forests, karst caverns and storm coasts.
- A few rare, explicitly defined anomalies where magic, ancient infrastructure or nonstandard physics has changed local conditions. These anomalies are geographically bounded until evidence supports a wider effect.

### Physical consistency gates

- Mountains influence wind, rainfall, snowpack, river sources and travel corridors.
- Rivers drain coherent watersheds and generally descend toward lakes, inland sinks or seas. Large rivers may have waterfalls, canyons, seasonal floods and engineered diversions, but these require terrain and history.
- Deserts need a cause: rain shadow, subtropical circulation, cold current, continentality, high elevation or a locally justified magical anomaly.
- Forests, grasslands, wetlands and tundra reflect climate, soil, water and disturbance.
- Ports need navigable water, shelter, infrastructure and a hinterland or strategic reason.
- Cities need water, food, waste management, labor, trade, defense and political history.
- A road cannot cross an impassable range without a pass, tunnel, bridge, engineered route or extraordinary mechanism.
- If the planetary scale changes, travel time, navigation, climate and political isolation must change with it.

## 2. Global atlas structure

The first world map uses six primary macro-regions. These are original atlas partitions, not canon names. Their boundaries are designed around physical geography, not arbitrary evenly sized polygons.

### I. The Crownward Reach — Empire and adjacent frontiers

**Purpose:** Place the manga's Empire within a large continental context without making it the entire world.

A broad, temperate-to-continental landmass with a fertile central basin, upland rims, river networks, agricultural plains, forested border belts and access to a major eastern ocean. The Empire controls a large connected core and many dependent territories, but terrain, logistics and political resistance limit its effective reach. The Capital and Great Canal occupy a heavily engineered heartland; Putra remains an arid, difficult region whose water infrastructure is a historical feat rather than evidence that deserts are easy to settle.

Proposed subregions for original expansion:
- **The Crown Basin:** central agricultural and administrative heartland.
- **The Slate Marches:** upland fortresses, quarry towns and old military roads.
- **The Bracken Verge:** wet temperate forest and dispersed frontier communities.
- **The Glasswind Interior:** dry basins, salt flats and flash-flood ravines; Putra's canon identity remains intact within this wider arid system.
- **The Eastern Littoral:** ports, shipyards, tidal flats and contested customs routes.
- **The Far Sere Coast:** storm-beaten capes, fishing settlements and dangerous coastal shallows.
- **The Old Canal Belt:** engineered waterways, towpaths, locks, aqueducts and abandoned labor settlements tied to Imperial infrastructure.
- **The Broken Banner Frontier:** a shifting belt of forts, refugee settlements, local polities and old rebellion scars.

These are proposed additions, not established manga names.

### II. The Sable Meridian — the western old kingdoms

A continent-sized region of broken highlands, deep river valleys, cold coasts and old stone cities. It contains several states whose histories predate the current Imperial era. Its politics revolve around water rights, mountain passes, succession, ancient charters and control of metal-bearing uplands.

Visual palette: iron-grey cliffs, wine-dark heath, pale limestone, pine-black ridges, copper roofs and river mist.

Architecture: stepped citadels fitted to cliffs; long-roofed timber halls in wet valleys; bridge-fortresses; dense market streets with covered arcades; monumental tomb roads cut into pale rock.

Possible states:
- **The Veyr Concord:** a league of river cities bound by contracts, hostage diplomacy and shared flood-control works.
- **The Ossuary Principalities:** small hereditary states built around ancestral crypts and quarry wealth; their public monuments make political claims through funerary architecture.
- **The Rimeward Holds:** upland polities controlling passes, mines and winter roads.
- **The Lethic Free Ports:** merchant republics that rent dock access, hire private fleets and publicly condemn piracy while discreetly profiting from it.

These states must have internal factions, minority communities, class conflicts and rival interpretations of their own history. No state is culturally uniform.

### III. The Verdant Furnace — southern equatorial regions

A humid belt of immense river forests, floodplains, volcanic soils, mangrove deltas and seasonal monsoon corridors. Travel depends on water levels, local knowledge, raised causeways, boats and dangerous dry-season bottlenecks. Some forests are biologically rich and politically organized; others are effectively controlled by Danger Beast territories.

Visual palette: wet jade, rust-red earth, black basalt, pollen gold, indigo shadow, white river mist and bright fungal light in a few biologically justified habitats.

Architecture: raised timber and stone platforms; steep rain-shedding roofs; shaded communal courtyards; flood-marked pillars; root-bridges where engineered and maintained; river markets; volcanic-stone walls; settlement plans that follow dry ridges rather than arbitrary grids.

Possible states and powers:
- **The Namaru River Compact:** several river polities sharing flood-control and navigation agreements; the compact fractures when upstream dams threaten downstream harvests.
- **The Ember Basin Houses:** city-states around volcanic uplands whose smithing depends on mineral access and carefully managed charcoal/heat resources.
- **The Keth Canopy Assemblies:** forest communities with layered territorial rights, seasonal councils and strict limits on extraction.
- **The Reed Throne of Oru:** a delta monarchy whose apparent wealth rests on fragile levees, labor obligations and control of grain storage.

The Ban Tribe and the manga's Southern Lands remain canon entities/regions where established; these new powers must not replace them. Their precise relative placement is pending source-map verification.

### IV. The Pale Expanse — northern continental shield

A cold, broad land of taiga, wind-scoured steppe, glacial lakes, permafrost fringes, mineral mountains and brief but intense growing seasons. The region is not empty: migration routes, winter settlements, reindeer-like herd analogues, river transport, seasonal fairs and fortified granaries structure its society.

Visual palette: blue-white snow glare, black spruce, iron-red lichen, smoke-grey skies, amber lamps and dark water under ice.

Architecture: low insulated halls, turf and stone roofs, deep-set windows, smoke channels, communal winter courts, timber palisades and raised storehouses. Wealth is displayed through labor-intensive carving, metalwork, imported dyes and the capacity to keep people alive through winter, not through tropical gardens.

Possible powers:
- **The Nine Hearth Confederacy:** a loose union of winter settlements that elects temporary war leaders but resists permanent central rule.
- **The Kharad Iron Synod:** mining towns governed by a religious-technical council that controls furnaces, tunnel rights and emergency food reserves.
- **The White Road Caravans:** mobile trading families whose authority comes from route knowledge, sled infrastructure and reciprocal winter shelters.
- **The Glass Glacier Enclaves:** isolated communities around glacial caves and mineral formations; their politics are defined by water sources and dangerous seasonal access.

The manga's Northern Tribe and related historical events remain distinct and must be placed according to source verification.

### V. The Thousand Shoals — eastern archipelagos and oceanic states

A vast network of islands, reef shelves, volcanic arcs, deep channels and monsoon seas. Maritime travel can be faster than overland travel but is never automatically safe. Navigation knowledge, currents, seasonal storms, harbor rights, shipbuilding materials and naval logistics are strategic resources.

Wakoku belongs to the manga's existing setting and must be integrated with care. Its Soukai and Tenrou nations and its Meihou tradition are canon-related anchors; the proposed archipelago around them is an expansion, not a claim that all the new islands appear in the manga.

Visual palette: basalt black, salt-white stone, storm teal, lacquer red, kelp green, bronze patina and pale volcanic ash.

Architecture: stepped harbor walls, timber-and-stone shipyards, tide stairs, windbreak alleys, terraced hillside towns, raised storm refuges, floating work platforms where technically justified, and sea-facing shrines with real maritime functions.

Possible original powers:
- **The Aokiri Sound League:** ports that regulate straits, pilotage, salvage and lighthouse maintenance.
- **The Nine-Keel Directorate:** a naval state where shipwright guilds and military admirals compete over timber, labor and dock capacity.
- **The Silt Crown Principalities:** delta and reef states whose boundaries shift with sediment and whose legal systems treat water access as a central right.
- **The Lantern Debt Houses:** maritime credit families whose loans bind ports, captains, monasteries and rulers across generations.

No new maritime power automatically controls Wakoku or rewrites its civil conflicts.

### VI. The Underdeep and High Quiet — exceptional environments

These are not separate planets. They are difficult-to-reach regions of Avarra that generate distinct cultures and ecological pressures.

**The Underdeep:** continental cave systems, karst labyrinths, buried aquifers, mineral vaults, fungal ecologies, ancient ventilation shafts and deep subterranean lakes. It is not one continuous hollow world. Cave networks are separated by solid rock, water, toxic air, narrow passages and collapses. Subterranean societies need credible food, light, heat, air exchange, waste management and trade.

**The High Quiet:** extreme plateaus and mountain basins where thin air, isolation, violent weather and unusual mineral deposits shape settlements. Some are monasteries, observatories, mining enclaves, fortress cities or independent polities. “Magic” must not be used to erase the practical costs of altitude unless the local mechanism explicitly does so.

## 3. Original non-Imperial power ecology

This world does not have one universal power system. It has interacting traditions with different premises, costs, training methods and failure conditions.

### A. Teigu and Shingu — canon-bound traditions

These remain tied to their established histories and known objects. They do not become a universal explanation for all extraordinary phenomena. The Empire's 48 Teigu history, losses, surviving relics and Shingu history must be tracked against manga sources.

### B. Meihou — Wakoku's regional tradition

Treat Meihou as a distinct craft/weapon tradition associated with Danger Beast materials and Wakoku, not as a mere renamed Teigu. The exact mechanics, history and limits require manga verification. New Meihou-like technologies outside Wakoku must be clearly labeled as analogous inventions rather than canon artifacts.

### C. Vowcraft — contract-bound power

An original tradition used by certain western oath courts, border wardens and outlaw fraternities. A practitioner establishes a formal vow with explicit scope, witnesses or proof conditions, a cost, and a failure consequence. The system cannot grant arbitrary abilities merely because a vow sounds dramatic. Vows are powerful because they constrain behavior, bind resources, create enforceable obligations or permit narrowly defined extraordinary effects.

Failure modes: ambiguous terms, invalid witnesses, conflicting obligations, exhausted reserves, broken conditions and deliberate legal exploitation. Vowcraft is not universally trusted; many societies consider it a predatory legal technology.

### D. Hearth Engines — industrial ritual engineering

An original technology that combines mechanical infrastructure, prepared materials, heat exchange, patterned conductors and repeatable procedures. It powers pumps, lifts, smelters, defensive gates, limited signal networks and a few dangerous field devices. It is not free energy. It requires fuel, maintenance, trained operators, replacement parts and waste handling.

Cultural effect: towns built around Hearth Engines develop maintenance guilds, spare-part markets, industrial quarters, worker hazards and political conflicts over fuel and repair monopolies.

### E. Rootsong — ecological symbiosis

An original tradition practiced by selected forest and wetland communities. It relies on long-term relationships with plants, fungi, animals and engineered habitats. It can support communication through chemical, vibrational or biological channels, but does not make an entire forest omniscient. It is slow, local, seasonal and vulnerable to drought, fire, disease and habitat destruction.

### F. Salt Memory — mineral and water inscription

An original coastal/Underdeep discipline that stores limited information or repeatable effects in treated salts, porous stone, brine layers or mineral lattices. It is finite, sensitive to contamination and temperature, and can degrade. It cannot read arbitrary minds or reproduce an entire person's memories without explicit, costly mechanisms.

### G. Grave Arithmetic — funerary accounting

An original tradition used by certain mortuary colleges and war-state institutions. It manages names, debts, legal identity, inheritance, corpse custody and the hazardous residues of certain deaths. Some practitioners can produce narrow effects involving remains or recorded obligations, but the system is not generic necromancy and does not grant unlimited resurrection.

### H. Beast-derived crafts

Danger Beast materials are worked through regional biological knowledge, smithing, alchemy, medicine and industrial processes. Results depend on species, anatomy, preparation, scarcity and craftsmanship. A beast's rare organ is not automatically a magical upgrade; its properties must be demonstrated and have trade-offs.

### Compatibility law

No system automatically converts into another. A Vowcraft binding cannot simply be powered by a Hearth Engine unless a designed interface exists. Rootsong cannot command every Danger Beast. Teigu do not obey all local magic traditions. Cross-system interactions are rare research problems, dangerous engineering projects or campaign discoveries, not universal conveniences.

## 4. Elves and other peoples — provisional expansion

Elves are allowed as an original people, but not as generic immortal humans with pointy ears.

**The Ilyr (working name)** are a set of related populations whose long-lived lineages developed in distinct forest, highland and subterranean environments. The term “Ilyr” is an outside umbrella label; different communities use their own names and may reject the classification.

Design principles:
- Long lifespans, if retained, do not imply uniform intelligence, wisdom, morality or political stability.
- Slow population replacement can create different inheritance rules, risk tolerance and responses to war, but these are pressures, not deterministic personality traits.
- Their settlements reflect ecology: canopy-supported districts, living windbreaks, stone-root foundations, watershed sanctuaries or high-altitude terracing where appropriate.
- They are not one kingdom. Include rival city-leagues, isolationists, trade houses, religious orders, displaced communities, mixed settlements and individuals who reject inherited custom.
- Their historical records can be incomplete, politically edited or contested. Longevity does not make memory perfect.
- Relations with humans, Danger Beasts and other peoples vary by region and history. Do not reduce them to “nature-loving forest people.”

Other original peoples may be considered only after ecology, reproduction, nutrition, material culture, political history and interspecies contact are designed. Species variety should create lived differences, not a checklist of fantasy tropes.

## 5. Cults, organizations and non-state powers

The world needs institutions that do not simply serve kings or empires. These are initial seeds, not a completed faction registry.

### The Choir of the Last Door
A funerary network that manages plague burials, battlefield recovery, disputed identities and unclaimed dead. Its members preserve records governments would prefer erased. Some branches are humane public servants; others sell identity changes, suppress evidence or weaponize burial rights.

### The Black Meridian
A transregional route-and-information fraternity whose members maintain dangerous roads, shelters, signal towers and maps. It sells reliable passage information but refuses to guarantee safety. Internal factions argue over whether the organization should remain neutral or intervene when rulers weaponize travel.

### The Red Ledger Houses
A loose network of credit, debt arbitration and insurance institutions. Their contracts can finance reconstruction or trap whole districts in inherited obligations. They are not a single omniscient conspiracy; houses compete, fail, forge records and sometimes default.

### The Hollow Saint Procession
A mobile religious movement that carries relics, medical stores and a portable tribunal between settlements abandoned by states. Its central doctrine is contested, and some local leaders exploit the movement's legitimacy.

### The Glass Orchard
A covert consortium of horticulturalists, poisoners, physicians and seed smugglers. Its activities range from preserving rare crops to targeted assassination. Different cells do not share perfect information.

### The Cartographers' Wake
A decentralized society of surveyors, river pilots, miners and ruin mappers. It protects some discoveries and sells others. Its maps record confidence, date and source instead of pretending every blank space is known.

### The Unmoored Court
A network of displaced nobles, deposed heirs, stateless legal scholars and mercenary negotiators. It can restore claims, broker exchanges or manufacture legal crises, but its members have incompatible goals.

### The Ashen Choir
A dangerous apocalyptic cult that interprets repeated ecological disasters as evidence that civilization must be dismantled. Some cells pursue sabotage; others provide aid and wait for collapse. The organization has no universal telepathic hive mind.

### The Quiet Knives
An assassin tradition organized through local schools, intermediaries and rival codes rather than one global guild. Contracts, verification, payment, safe houses, evidence disposal, collateral risk and succession all matter. Some branches reject political assassination; others specialize in it.

### The Deep Bell Compact
A loose alliance of Underdeep communities responsible for maintaining warning bells, ventilation shafts, flood gates and safe routes. A neglected shaft or broken bell can kill an entire settlement. Their political power comes from controlling life-support infrastructure.

Each organization requires a dossier: founding conditions, membership, internal divisions, recruitment, resources, operating territory, logistics, public reputation, secret knowledge, rivals, allies, doctrine, succession, failure modes and current agenda.

## 6. Indirect consequence law

The world must model causal propagation rather than treating actions as isolated visual events.

A punch is not merely “impact happens.” Depending on the actual situation:
- The target's body accelerates or rotates; footing, balance, grip and orientation change.
- The target may collide with a wall, table, railing or another person.
- The collision transfers load to the structure; a weak joint can loosen, a brittle surface can crack, or a supported object can fall.
- Noise may alert nearby people, frighten animals, interrupt a conversation or mask another sound.
- Blood, dropped tools, displaced furniture or damaged clothing may become evidence.
- A witness may misunderstand what happened and pass on an inaccurate report.
- The action consumes time, alters the participants' positions and creates new vulnerabilities.
- The consequence can propagate further only when there is a plausible physical, social or informational path.

Use a **causal fan-out check** after meaningful actions:
1. Direct physical result.
2. Secondary physical interactions.
3. Body-state changes (injury, balance, fatigue, grip, pain, breathing).
4. Environmental changes (objects, surfaces, structure, light, sound, scent, debris).
5. Witness and information consequences.
6. Resource, legal, political, ecological or economic consequences where relevant.
7. Delayed effects and recovery/repair requirements.
8. Explicitly reject speculative cascades with no credible path.

Not every action needs a paragraph enumerating all eight. The simulation should calculate/reason through the relevant branches and narrate the consequences that are consequential, observable or decision-relevant. “Almost always an indirect consequence” means check for it; it does not mean invent an earthquake after a tavern punch.

## 7. Macro-history scaffold — provisional, not canon

Avarra's original history should be built in causal eras rather than a list of disconnected ancient empires.

- **The Landform Age:** tectonic uplift, volcanic arcs, drainage basins and long-term climate establish the physical regions. This is natural history, not a mythic war unless local cultures later interpret it as one.
- **The First Routes:** early peoples develop seasonal crossings, river navigation, highland passes, coastal piloting and cave-to-surface exchange. Different regions develop different transport and food systems.
- **The Age of Stored Surplus:** irrigation, granaries, metalworking, animal husbandry, forest management and port infrastructure enable permanent cities. Those who control storage and transport gain leverage.
- **The Age of Oaths and Engines:** several unrelated traditions emerge—Vowcraft, Hearth Engines, Rootsong and Salt Memory—under different cultural conditions. Their institutions compete and sometimes exchange techniques, but no global school unifies them.
- **The Fractured Crown Centuries:** western river leagues, northern holds, southern basin houses and maritime powers rise and fall through resource disputes, succession crises, epidemics, ecological damage and logistics.
- **The Imperial Millennium:** the Empire's own history unfolds as established by manga canon, with the First Emperor, Teigu, later civil conflict, corruption and rebellion. Its chronology must not be retrofitted to make it the cause of every global development.
- **The Present Interwoven Age:** multiple states, organizations and non-state powers remain active. Trade, migration, espionage and war create contact, but distances and political isolation still matter.

The precise dates, names, dynasties and wars for the original eras remain to be authored in dedicated history records. This scaffold is a causal framework, not the finished timeline.

## 8. Global-scale realism

A large world must not become a world where everything is connected to everything else.

- Distance creates latency.
- Mountains and seas create barriers and specialized intermediaries.
- Language, law, calendars, weights, currencies and navigation conventions differ.
- States have limited budgets, manpower, information and administrative reach.
- Trade networks transmit goods, disease, techniques, rumors and debt, but unevenly.
- A war in one basin may alter grain prices elsewhere if trade routes and markets connect them; it does not automatically alter an isolated mountain monastery.
- Some cultures will have no meaningful contact with the Empire for generations.
- A powerful technology may remain geographically contained because its inputs, training, infrastructure or doctrine cannot be reproduced elsewhere.
- A cult can be locally influential and globally irrelevant.
- A regional disaster becomes global only through a defensible chain of ecological, economic, migratory or political consequences.

## 9. Atlas build order

1. Establish a global map grid, projection, scale and major land/water outlines.
2. Place canon geography after chapter-level source verification.
3. Define geology, elevation and tectonic/volcanic systems.
4. Derive climate, prevailing winds, precipitation and hydrology.
5. Establish biomes, Danger Beast ecology and resource regions.
6. Place routes, ports, passes, river crossings, mines and chokepoints.
7. Design nation borders, states, dependencies, disputed zones and isolation.
8. Author major cities and towns with district plans, street networks, silhouettes, colors, materials and infrastructure.
9. Create faction and institutional histories linked to geography.
10. Add procedural expansion rules for the un-authored spaces between stable landmarks.
11. Run map, logistics, ecology, chronology and causal-continuity checks.
12. Record every new canon boundary, original addition and unresolved dependency.

## 10. Open decisions

- The planet's final name, radius, gravity, day length, year length and moon system.
- Whether the 2.4-Earth-surface design target survives physical consistency checks.
- Exact placement of all manga geography and the Empire's relationship to each macro-region.
- How the manga's events align with the larger world's calendar.
- Which new peoples are present from the beginning and which remain remote/rumored.
- Whether any off-world locations are real and accessible; interplanetary travel must be a rare, explicitly governed mechanism, not a casual interruption.
- The final count and names of continents, nations, cities, languages, religions, cults and organizations.
- The detailed history of the First Emperor and the Empire's official name. Do not fill source gaps and call them canon.

The world should become enormous through connected geography, independent histories, competing systems and persistent consequences—not by maximizing the number of nouns in a lore book.
