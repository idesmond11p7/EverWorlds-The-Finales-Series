# 16. Desert Civilizations and Water Politics
Status: original worldbuilding seeds. These are not Akame ga Kill canon. They are regional prototypes to connect to the physical geography map after watersheds and climate are authored.

## Design rule
“Desert civilization” is a climate category, not an ethnicity or shared culture. Each society below has a distinct water budget, settlement logic, material palette, economy, institutions, conflict pattern and aesthetic. Names and locations remain provisional until the planetary map establishes where each watershed and trade route belongs.

## 1. The Vaskh Stair-Cities
- **Terrain:** deep sandstone canyons cut by violent seasonal floods.
- **Water:** upstream catch dams, cistern terraces and controlled spillways. A blocked spillway can destroy entire lower districts.
- **Settlement:** stacked neighborhoods on canyon walls, connected by switchback stairs, rope lifts and bridges with removable spans.
- **Economy:** stonework, flood engineering, caravan repairs and canyon-grown crops.
- **Government:** competing water courts whose authority depends on maintaining dams; engineers can overrule noble families during flood alerts.
- **Aesthetic:** rust-red stone, chalk-white flood marks, dark timber gates and hanging bridges.
- **Pressure:** upstream sabotage, silt buildup, and disputes over who pays for repairs.
- **Distinctive play:** a safe route in dry weather may become impassable in minutes; water levels and bridge state persist.

## 2. The Nacre Wind Ports
- **Terrain:** cool coastal desert with fog-bearing winds and salt cliffs.
- **Water:** fog nets, condensation towers and cistern roofs; water production collapses during still, dry winds.
- **Settlement:** narrow shaded streets behind wind-catching facades; ports descend through cliff elevators to sheltered coves.
- **Economy:** salt, glass, navigation instruments, ship repair and water export.
- **Government:** civic water accountants elected by districts; smugglers compete to falsify water ledgers.
- **Aesthetic:** pale mineral plaster, blue-black glazed tiles, woven wind baffles and translucent shell-like screens.
- **Pressure:** drought years, harbor blockades and the danger of overharvesting coastal fog.
- **Distinctive play:** the wind direction changes which alleys are cool, which roofs collect water and which harbor entrances are safe.

## 3. The Qadresh Moving Compact
- **Terrain:** open dune sea crossed by seasonal grazing corridors.
- **Water:** mapped wells, negotiated access, portable cisterns and rare deep springs.
- **Settlement:** mobile encampments arranged around windbreaks; permanent markets appear at seasonal convergence points.
- **Economy:** animal breeding, route guidance, escort work, textiles and caravan brokerage.
- **Government:** a compact of route-keeping families; leadership rotates when routes or water agreements change.
- **Aesthetic:** layered earth dyes, flexible frames, patterned shade cloth and portable objects designed for repair.
- **Pressure:** route monopolies, disputed wells and climate shifts that invalidate inherited migration calendars.
- **Distinctive play:** the “right road” changes with season, herd movement, water reports and political permission.

## 4. The White Pilgrimage of Serekh
- **Terrain:** dazzling salt flats surrounding a mineral spring and seasonal inland lake.
- **Water:** spring-fed reservoirs and strict visitor allotments; the lake is unreliable.
- **Settlement:** low white buildings with covered passages and underground sleeping halls; navigation relies on dark stone markers and night beacons.
- **Economy:** pilgrimage, salt, ceramics, lodging, medical care and legal arbitration.
- **Government:** sanctuary custodians share power with merchants who fund reservoir maintenance.
- **Aesthetic:** chalk, polished saltstone, black basalt markers, deep indigo interiors and restrained geometric relief.
- **Pressure:** pilgrimage surges can exhaust supplies; rival custodians disagree over whether outsiders receive water.
- **Distinctive play:** daytime glare obscures distance; night travel is easier to orient but exposes travelers to cold and ambush.

## 5. The Belowwater Houses of Urum
- **Terrain:** a dry plateau above a deep, slowly replenished aquifer.
- **Water:** carefully monitored wells, infiltration galleries and ancient cistern chambers.
- **Settlement:** partly buried city with ventilation towers, cool courts and access shafts; lower districts are abandoned when water tables fall.
- **Economy:** ceramics, subterranean fungi, mineral dyes, archive preservation and water measurement.
- **Government:** household status is tied to maintenance obligations rather than raw ownership of wells; falsifying aquifer readings is a severe crime.
- **Aesthetic:** ochre exteriors, cool blue interiors, perforated stone screens, soft reflected light and sound-dampening passageways.
- **Pressure:** aquifer depletion, illegal drilling and unequal access to ventilation.
- **Distinctive play:** a shortcut through the lower city may become unsafe after a water-table or structural change.

## 6. The Kharz Blackglass Dominion
- **Terrain:** volcanic desert with basalt fields, ash plains and geothermal fissures.
- **Water:** deep wells and condensation systems powered by geothermal heat; some water is mineralized and needs treatment.
- **Settlement:** low basalt structures clustered around heat exchangers, with industrial districts separated from living quarters by ash barriers.
- **Economy:** forged alloys, glass, ceramics, geothermal machinery and mineral extraction.
- **Government:** competing foundry houses regulated by a public heat-and-water board.
- **Aesthetic:** matte black stone, copper piping, amber glass, ash-white work cloth and visible repair seams.
- **Pressure:** toxic vents, mine collapse, heat-system failure and labor conflict.
- **Distinctive play:** a broken heat main can change work schedules, water treatment and neighborhood safety.

## 7. The Saffron Route League
- **Terrain:** a chain of oasis basins separated by long dune crossings.
- **Water:** each oasis controls its own wells; the League survives by standardizing caravan schedules and water-credit records.
- **Settlement:** market towns with multiple gates, freight yards, animal courts and separate quarters for seasonal caravans.
- **Economy:** brokerage, maps, pack animals, preserved food, translation and insurance against route loss.
- **Government:** a council of oasis delegates; no member can command the whole League without a negotiated emergency compact.
- **Aesthetic:** ochre brick, green-blue wayfinding marks, painted freight seals and large shaded counting halls.
- **Pressure:** forged route records, competing tolls, drought and unequal leverage between large and small oases.
- **Distinctive play:** a route can be physically open but commercially closed because water credit or safe-conduct has been revoked.

## 8. The Orison of Cold Stars
- **Terrain:** high-latitude cold desert with clear skies, thin vegetation and severe night temperature drops.
- **Water:** seasonal snowmelt, protected ice stores and deep springs.
- **Settlement:** compact insulated observatory towns with sun-facing stone courts, underground food stores and distant beacon stations.
- **Economy:** navigation, astronomical calendars, precision lenses, cold preservation and long-distance message relays.
- **Government:** observatory houses compete over calendar standards and the right to certify navigation tables.
- **Aesthetic:** dark blue stone, pale felt, bronze instruments, narrow sun slots and high night-sky towers.
- **Pressure:** blizzards, unreliable melt seasons and political manipulation of travel calendars.
- **Distinctive play:** a route may be visible under clear stars yet lethal due to wind chill, ice crust or lack of shelter.

## 9. The Red Silt Mandate
- **Terrain:** a desert margin around a river that floods irregularly from distant mountains.
- **Water:** seasonal flood basins, canals, sediment management and grain reserves.
- **Settlement:** broad mud-brick towns on raised platforms; old districts are marked by layers of floodline plaster.
- **Economy:** grain, reeds, brick, irrigation engineering, river transport and floodplain livestock.
- **Government:** basin councils allocate water by measured land and maintenance labor, a rule constantly contested by powerful estates.
- **Aesthetic:** red silt brick, white lime flood marks, reed canopies, painted canal gates and broad public courtyards.
- **Pressure:** upstream dams, salinization, canal neglect and grain-price shocks.
- **Distinctive play:** a harvest failure affects tax receipts, repairs, food prices, migration and political stability over time.

## 10. The Glass Dune Monasteries
- **Terrain:** a wind-polished desert plateau with glassy mineral deposits and isolated springs.
- **Water:** monastery cisterns and tightly rationed spring rights; visitors must contribute labor or supplies.
- **Settlement:** small fortified monastic cities connected by marked walking routes, each built around a different discipline and architectural rule.
- **Economy:** copying, healing, glasscraft, mediation, astronomy and specialist education.
- **Government:** independent houses share a pilgrimage code but disagree sharply over doctrine and the use of supernatural practices.
- **Aesthetic:** each house has its own silhouette: stepped towers, circular courts, carved wind organs, buried halls or mirrorless dark sanctuaries. No single “monk city” template.
- **Pressure:** doctrinal schisms, resource scarcity and the temptation to use forbidden methods during emergencies.
- **Distinctive play:** sanctuary does not imply safety everywhere; local rules, political neutrality and hospitality obligations differ by house.

## 11. The Mournwater Nomads
- **Terrain:** a salt basin whose shallow lake appears only after unusual rains.
- **Water:** brackish springs and seasonal lake collection, with treatment requiring charcoal, ceramics and labor.
- **Settlement:** seasonal camps and floating reed platforms during wet periods; dry-season routes follow mineral crusts and old shoreline markers.
- **Economy:** salt harvesting, mineral pigments, fish when available, reedwork and seasonal labor.
- **Government:** route assemblies convened at the shoreline; agreements expire when the lake changes, unless renewed explicitly.
- **Aesthetic:** pale reed, dark salt-stained leather, red mineral pigment and objects designed to be dismantled.
- **Pressure:** toxic dust, uncertain lake cycles and outsiders claiming permanent ownership over a shifting resource.
- **Distinctive play:** the map itself changes seasonally; old paths remain as records but may no longer be safe.

## 12. The Deep Ember Caravans
- **Terrain:** broken desert highlands with caves, fumaroles and mineral springs.
- **Water:** spring enclaves connected by dangerous passes; route knowledge is guarded as a trade secret.
- **Settlement:** caravans use portable heat shields and modular camps; permanent towns occupy cave mouths and sheltered shelves.
- **Economy:** rare minerals, heat-resistant ceramics, medicinal salts, escort work and cave exploration.
- **Government:** independent caravan families share emergency rescue obligations but compete fiercely over prospecting rights.
- **Aesthetic:** dark red cloth, ceramic armor plates, smoke-blackened copper and compact portable shrines.
- **Pressure:** cave gas, unstable roofs, disputed claims and the risk that extraction poisons a spring.
- **Distinctive play:** mining changes airflow, water quality and cave stability; profitable extraction can make a route unsafe later.

## Cross-civilization systems
### Water is not interchangeable
A fog net, a fossil aquifer, a seasonal lake and a mountain-fed canal have different capacities, failure modes and repair times. Water should have source, quality, quantity, access rights, storage, transport cost and seasonal reliability.

### Trade creates dependence, not sameness
One city exports glass; another exports grain; another controls route information. Trade produces shared standards and political leverage but does not erase language, architecture, religion or local law.

### Desert travel is route-specific
Travel risk depends on water intervals, shade, weather, route knowledge, load, animal condition, permits, escorts and seasonal conditions. A route may be geographically passable but socially forbidden or economically unaffordable.

### Aesthetic is structural
Architecture follows shade, wind, heat, material, defense, water and labor. Clothing and equipment follow the same pressures. Use palette and ornament only after the functional logic is clear.

### Supernatural exceptions require limits
A magical well, weather-binding rite or heatless engine is allowed only if it has defined access, cost, failure mode, social control and consequences. It cannot silently make scarcity irrelevant.

## Integration checklist
Before placing any civilization on the world map:
1. Choose its latitude, elevation and climate.
2. Establish watershed, water source and seasonal reliability.
3. Establish local food, materials and energy.
4. Draw the settlement's route connections and supply intervals.
5. Define the water institution and its political conflicts.
6. Link it to at least two neighboring societies through a concrete exchange or dispute.
7. Define one ongoing environmental or logistical pressure.
8. Establish visual grammar from materials and climate.
9. Assign stable IDs and mark all current names as provisional until mapped.
