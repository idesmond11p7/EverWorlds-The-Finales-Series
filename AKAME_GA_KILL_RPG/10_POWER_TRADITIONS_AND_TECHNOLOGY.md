# 10 — Power Traditions, Technology and Cross-System Limits

**Status:** Original systems design except the explicitly canon-bound Teigu, Shingu and Meihou references. Exact canon mechanics still require chapter-level sourcing. The new systems do not replace the manga's systems.

## 1. Shared design contract

Every power tradition is a constrained practice embedded in a culture and material economy. A power is not a list of arbitrary special moves. It has a theory, training path, enabling conditions, costs, failure modes, observable evidence, social institutions and counters.

For every ability, record:
- what the practitioner can demonstrably do;
- what the practitioner cannot do;
- target, range, area, duration and precision;
- preparation and execution time;
- physical/material requirements;
- fatigue, injury, resource or social cost;
- conditions that interrupt or degrade it;
- sensory signs and evidence left behind;
- training, maintenance and failure recovery;
- interaction with terrain, weather, armor, infrastructure and other powers;
- what is actually known versus what practitioners merely believe.

A tradition can contain false theories. The simulation distinguishes an effect that works from the explanation its practitioners give.

## 2. Canon-bound traditions

### 2.1 Teigu
**Status:** Manga-canon tradition. Do not rewrite the known set or its histories without evidence.

Research tasks:
- register every Teigu with manga name, wielder, first appearance, mechanism, demonstrated limits, known cost, transfer/destruction status and last known state;
- distinguish stated origin lore from fan interpretation;
- record the conditions for activation and whether a user can operate it immediately;
- record interactions with other Teigu only where demonstrated;
- separate a weapon's named ability from assumptions about its maximum possible scale.

The original Empire history is not a universal origin story for every power on the planet. Teigu are historically important, not the explanation for all magic.

### 2.2 Shingu
**Status:** Manga-canon term; exact history and mechanics need direct verification.

Maintain a separate registry from Teigu. Similar purpose does not mean identical construction, power ceiling, safety, compatibility or political status. Do not create a universal “Teigu tier” and use it to rank unrelated systems.

### 2.3 Meihou
**Status:** Regional Wakoku-related tradition requiring source verification.

Treat Meihou as a distinct cultural and craft tradition associated in secondary sources with Danger Beast materials. Verify its terminology, examples, construction and combat use directly. New equipment inspired by the general idea must be marked original, not called a canon Meihou.

## 3. Original tradition A — Vowcraft

**Core proposition:** A formally witnessed commitment can create a bounded obligation with an extraordinary effect, but only when terms, cost and proof conditions are explicit.

### Practice
1. State the obligation in unambiguous language.
2. Define scope, duration, exceptions and proof of completion.
3. Specify the cost or resource at risk.
4. Establish witnesses, seals or other accepted validation.
5. Bind only the participants and targets that the mechanism actually supports.
6. Record the agreement in a form that can be audited.

### Capabilities
- Strengthen adherence to a narrow self-imposed condition.
- Make a bounded promise costly to violate.
- Attach a limited effect to a defined trigger.
- Create evidence of compliance or breach when the contract's mechanism supports it.

### Cannot do
- Force any person to accept a vow without a mechanism.
- Grant an undefined power merely because the wording is dramatic.
- Guarantee that a court can enforce an agreement in a distant jurisdiction.
- Read minds or prove intent from a signature alone.
- Remove physical costs, coercion, loopholes or ambiguity.

### Costs and failures
Ambiguous wording, invalid witnesses, coercion, competing vows, exhausted reserves, broken seals and conflicting jurisdiction can weaken or invalidate an effect. Sophisticated users exploit legal ambiguities, making interpreters and precedent as important as combat practitioners.

### Social consequences
Vow courts, oath brokers, contract schools, anti-coercion movements and black markets for forged witnesses emerge. Poor people may be pressured into dangerous contracts. A society that uses Vowcraft heavily must develop laws about informed consent and unequal bargaining power.

## 4. Original tradition B — Hearth Engines

**Core proposition:** Prepared material circuits and mechanical infrastructure produce repeatable, bounded effects by converting fuel, pressure, heat, motion or stored charge.

### Uses
- water pumping and pressure control;
- lifts, winches and industrial movement;
- controlled smelting and furnace regulation;
- limited alarms and signal relays;
- defensive gates and short-duration barriers where the mechanism is explicitly defined;
- survey and measurement instruments.

### Infrastructure
Hearth Engines require fuel, trained operators, seals, replacement parts, maintenance schedules, waste management, safe ventilation and supply chains. A machine can be powerful while remaining immobile and vulnerable.

### Limits
- Output depends on input energy and material tolerances.
- Heat, pressure, wear and corrosion accumulate.
- Complex systems fail at joints, valves, seals and control points.
- Field use is harder than workshop use.
- Damage can injure operators and bystanders.
- A destroyed component may halt an entire network if spares are scarce.

### Political effects
Maintenance guilds gain leverage. Cities create safety inspectors, repair depots, spare-part standards, labor disputes and industrial pollution. Military use competes with civilian water, transport and heat needs.

## 5. Original tradition C — Rootsong

**Core proposition:** Specialized practitioners interpret and sometimes influence biological signals through long-term ecological relationships, cultivation and carefully prepared stimuli.

### Possible effects
- detect limited plant stress or recent disturbance;
- encourage a plant's growth within biological limits;
- use cultivated organisms as local warning systems;
- communicate limited signals through known animal/plant relationships;
- manage symbiotic habitats and selected Danger Beast-adjacent ecologies.

### Limits
- Signals are local and ambiguous.
- Species need suitable conditions and time.
- Drought, fire, disease, contamination and habitat fragmentation degrade the network.
- Plants do not report exact names, motives or conversations.
- A forest is not a single hive mind.
- Rootsong cannot automatically control animals or all Danger Beasts.

### Social consequences
Practitioners require land access and long-term stewardship. Political conflicts arise over who owns living networks, whether cutting a tree is sabotage, and whether outsiders can use cultivated warning groves.

## 6. Original tradition D — Salt Memory

**Core proposition:** Prepared mineral, salt and porous-stone structures can retain limited patterns or trigger repeatable effects under specific conditions.

### Uses
- durable marks and tamper evidence;
- bounded storage of a short signal or pattern;
- limited environmental sensing;
- preservation of records resistant to ordinary moisture;
- local warning devices.

### Limits
- Capacity is finite.
- Temperature, contamination, water chemistry and abrasion can degrade a record.
- Storage does not imply comprehension.
- It cannot record all sensory detail or a full human memory by default.
- Repeated use can saturate or damage the medium.
- Verification requires instruments and trained readers.

### Social consequences
Salt memory creates archive guilds, counterfeiters, conservation practices and disputes over whether a damaged record remains admissible evidence.

## 7. Original tradition E — Grave Arithmetic

**Core proposition:** A funerary discipline that combines legal identity, corpse custody, names, obligations and limited interaction with hazardous residues left by certain deaths.

### Ordinary functions
- preserve identity and burial records;
- track remains through war and epidemic;
- establish inheritance and disputed kinship evidence;
- manage quarantine and corpse-handling protocols;
- prevent fraudulent reuse of the dead's legal identity.

### Exceptional functions
Some trained practitioners can produce narrow effects from prepared remains or recorded obligations. Every effect must have a specific substrate, duration, risk and failure mode.

### Limits
- Death is not casually reversible.
- A name alone does not summon a soul or reveal truth.
- Remains degrade and can be contaminated or misidentified.
- Legal identity is not the same as personal memory.
- Grave Arithmetic does not grant unlimited resurrection, interrogation or mind reading.

### Social consequences
Mortuary colleges can protect vulnerable families or become coercive state institutions. The same record that proves a person existed can be used to seize their property.

## 8. Original tradition F — Beastcraft

Beastcraft is not one global magic system. It is a family of regional material practices: butchery, tanning, bone-working, venom refinement, organ preservation, medicine, armor, tools and rare bounded effects.

A material's property must be demonstrated. If a hide resists heat, define the conditions and limits. If a gland yields a toxin, define extraction, stability, dose, delivery, antidote uncertainty and ecological cost. Do not grant a harvested organ every ability the living beast possessed.

## 9. Cross-system compatibility matrix

Default relation is **incompatible until demonstrated**. Shared visual effects do not establish shared mechanisms.

| Pair | Default relation | Plausible research interface |
|---|---|---|
| Teigu ↔ Vowcraft | Unknown / no automatic link | A contract governing custody or activation, not changing the Teigu's intrinsic rules |
| Teigu ↔ Hearth Engine | Unknown / no automatic link | Mechanical containment or measurement that does not assume control over the Teigu |
| Meihou ↔ Beastcraft | Potential material overlap | Shared species/material research, with regional terminology kept distinct |
| Vowcraft ↔ Hearth Engine | No automatic conversion | A vow-bound operator procedure with explicit mechanical safeguards |
| Rootsong ↔ Beastcraft | Local ecological overlap | Species monitoring, cultivation and material sustainability |
| Salt Memory ↔ Grave Arithmetic | Possible recordkeeping interface | A validated inscription method for burial identity, not automatic soul storage |
| Rootsong ↔ Salt Memory | No assumed compatibility | Controlled experiments on chemical/vibrational patterns |
| Any system ↔ off-world phenomenon | Unknown | Requires a separately defined access mechanism and reproducible evidence |

## 10. Power balance without a single tier list

Do not rank all practitioners on one ladder. Use operational profiles:
- immediate lethality;
- range and area;
- preparation;
- stamina/material cost;
- durability and defense;
- stealth and detectability;
- mobility and terrain dependence;
- reliability under stress;
- countermeasure availability;
- strategic logistics and political consequences.

A person can dominate in a prepared workshop and be nearly helpless in rain, underwater, in a crowded market or without spare parts. Another can be tactically modest but strategically powerful because they maintain a bridge network or control a vital medicine.

## 11. Ability test protocol

For every new ability:
1. Describe a low-stakes demonstration.
2. Describe a failure caused by a normal constraint.
3. Describe a countermeasure available to a competent opponent.
4. Describe the cost and recovery time.
5. Describe what evidence an observer can detect.
6. Test use in rain, darkness, confined space and crowded surroundings when relevant.
7. Test indirect consequences to nearby people, objects and infrastructure.
8. Test whether the ability can be exploited for mundane economic or political purposes.
9. Define what the system still cannot explain.
10. Record all claims as demonstrated, inferred, rumored or false.
