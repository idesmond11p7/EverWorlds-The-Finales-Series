# 09 — Nation and Peoples Dossiers: First Expansion Batch

**Status:** Original worldbuilding. Every name, institution, history and political arrangement below is an expansion proposal, not *Akame ga Kill!* manga canon. Boundaries remain provisional until the global atlas exists.

## 1. Nation model

A state is a set of institutions and territorial claims, not a colored patch. Each state must define:
- settlement and population distribution;
- food, water, energy and material supply;
- transport network and seasonal bottlenecks;
- government, law, taxation and enforcement;
- military manpower, equipment, training and logistics;
- internal class, regional, religious and ideological conflicts;
- languages and translation needs;
- foreign relations, migration and information channels;
- ecology, hazards and resource limits;
- historic events that explain its present shape;
- realistic conditions for fragmentation, reform, conquest or collapse.

The numbers below are not invented as exact census figures. Population estimates should wait until territory, agricultural productivity, settlement density and transport are mapped.

## 2. The Veyr Concord — river cities bound by flood law

**Government:** Treaty league of river cities. The Concord is not a unified kingdom; member cities retain local courts, taxes and militia.  
**Core territory:** The middle river valleys of the Sable Meridian, where seasonal floods make coordinated engineering more valuable than permanent conquest.  
**Founding cause:** A catastrophic flood destroyed several competing river towns. Survivors built shared levees and a common rule for upstream water releases. The Concord began as a practical water compact and became a political institution.  
**Capital function:** Ors Vey hosts the assembly and the Seven-Rain Archive. It is not the sole source of authority; member cities keep their own copies of treaty records.  
**Economy:** Grain, barges, pottery, textiles, bridge tolls, flood engineering, paper and recordkeeping. Wealth depends on navigable water and functioning embankments.  
**Military:** River patrols, militia, engineers, bridge guards and hired specialists. The Concord can move supplies quickly along rivers but struggles to project force into mountains or across oceans.  
**Law:** Water obligations are central. A city that releases a damaging flood can face compensation claims, trade restrictions and political isolation. Disputes often concern proof, not merely guilt.  
**Internal conflict:** Upstream cities want freedom to expand reservoirs; downstream farmers fear losing water. Wealthy levee districts resist paying for poorer districts' repairs.  
**Information:** Water gauges and courier boats carry urgent reports; winter ice, storms and conflict delay them. Records may be copied incorrectly or manipulated by local officials.  
**Visual grammar:** Pale limestone, river-slate, copper roofs, flood marks, bridge arches, covered markets and archive courtyards. Public buildings show the height of past floods.  
**Failure conditions:** Multiple levee failures, treaty breach by the largest upstream city, grain shortage, or a flood archive destroyed during a succession dispute.  
**Player entry points:** Survey a failing levee, mediate a water dispute, escort a flood engineer, uncover falsified gauges, or decide which district receives scarce repair materials.

## 3. The Rimeward Holds — mountain pass confederation

**Government:** Autonomous fortress-holds linked by pass treaties, winter shelter obligations and rotating councils. No single hold can order all the others indefinitely.  
**Core territory:** High valleys and passes of the northern Sable Meridian, with mineral ridges and short growing seasons.  
**Founding cause:** The holds learned that isolated fortresses starve when snow closes roads. Their political unity grew from shared granaries, rescue obligations and agreements on avalanche control.  
**Economy:** Iron, stone, salt, wool, preserved foods, pack animals, pass tolls and skilled construction. Imports include grain, medicines, fine textiles and some fuel.  
**Military:** Mountain infantry, scouts, engineers, pass guards and small specialist units. Their defensive advantage is terrain knowledge; their weakness is limited food and difficulty sustaining large campaigns away from home.  
**Law:** Shelter and rescue are customary obligations. Refusing a traveler during an officially declared storm emergency can be prosecuted; using a shelter to spy on a rival hold is a recurring controversy.  
**Internal conflict:** Mine owners want more tunnels; valley communities fear water contamination and landslides. Younger residents seek lowland work while elders see outmigration as strategic decline.  
**Information:** Signal fires and bells work only under certain visibility conditions. Messages can be delayed by weather; false alarms can exhaust emergency stores.  
**Visual grammar:** Cold granite, pine-black timber, white limewash, rust-red roof tiles, covered lanes, avalanche walls and deep-set windows.  
**Failure conditions:** Two consecutive poor harvests, loss of a critical pass, collapse of a major mine, or a dispute over winter stores that ends the shelter treaty.  
**Player entry points:** Repair a signal station, investigate a mine collapse, negotiate safe passage, or expose a council that hid a food deficit.

## 4. The Namaru River Compact — upstream and downstream in permanent negotiation

**Government:** River cities and floodplain communities delegate to a compact council. Its authority is strongest over navigation and shared floodworks and weakest in local family law or remote forest settlements.  
**Core territory:** A southern river basin with monsoon seasons, raised settlements and navigable tributaries.  
**Founding cause:** Repeated flood and drought cycles taught communities that a dam, diversion or cleared forest upstream can ruin farms downstream. The compact's legitimacy rests on shared measurements and emergency labor.  
**Economy:** Grain, fish, boatbuilding, medicinal plants, timber, woven goods, river transport and controlled extraction of certain Danger Beast materials.  
**Military:** Patrol boats, levee guards, local militias and river engineers. It has excellent knowledge of channels but limited capacity in dry uplands and deep forest.  
**Law:** Upstream construction requires notice and impact review; emergency exceptions exist but are politically abused. Communities can demand repair labor or compensation after a proven downstream harm.  
**Internal conflict:** Upstream cities want hydro-engineering and expansion; downstream villages oppose sediment loss and altered seasonal floods. Merchants seek reliable navigation even when ecological flows suffer.  
**Information:** Water levels are measured locally; readings travel by boat and signal station. A missing gauge can create uncertainty for several settlements.  
**Visual grammar:** Wet jade, red clay, black water, pale timber, steep rain roofs, flood archives, raised markets and broad public stairs to docks.  
**Failure conditions:** Upstream dam failure, corruption in water measurements, crop disease, or the compact splitting into competing channel authorities.  
**Player entry points:** Track a toxic discharge, repair a flood gate, negotiate a drought allocation, or protect a survey team from a faction that benefits from false measurements.

## 5. The Reed Throne of Oru — delta monarchy on borrowed ground

**Government:** Hereditary monarch supported by the Water Office, grain stewards, temple estates and boat guilds. The crown's power depends on their cooperation.  
**Core territory:** A delta of shifting channels, reed marshes and engineered mounds.  
**Founding cause:** A dynasty gained legitimacy by coordinating levees and grain stores after several coastal storms. Later rulers transformed emergency duties into hereditary labor obligations.  
**Economy:** Rice-like wetland grains, fish, salt, reed products, boats, textiles and delta tolls. Grain exports fund the court; grain shortages threaten its legitimacy.  
**Military:** River patrols, levy infantry, palace guards and engineers. The state can close sluices and control routes, but widespread levee failure can trap its own forces.  
**Law:** Water maintenance is a civic duty in theory and an unequal burden in practice. Wealthy estates often pay money instead of labor; poor households lose days or land.  
**Internal conflict:** Reformers demand paid maintenance and transparent grain reserves. The court claims that changing obligations would endanger the whole delta. Some boat guilds quietly support reform while profiting from the current system.  
**Information:** Sluice records and granary ledgers are political secrets. Rumors travel quickly through boat crews but may be distorted by competing districts.  
**Visual grammar:** Reed gold, brine silver, lacquer red, mudbrick, raised granaries, tide stairs and signal poles above storm water.  
**Failure conditions:** Storm surge combined with neglected levees, saltwater intrusion, a fraudulent grain inventory, or a labor refusal at critical sluices.  
**Player entry points:** Expose grain theft, protect a flood survey, help a district resist forced labor, or choose between a palace order and an emergency evacuation.

## 6. The Keth Canopy Assemblies — forest territories without a single forest king

**Government:** A network of local assemblies linked by seasonal councils and negotiated resource rights. Some communities are egalitarian; others have hereditary stewards or merchant oligarchies.  
**Core territory:** Moist forests, raised ridges and tributary valleys near the Verdant Furnace.  
**Founding cause:** Settlements learned that uncontrolled logging, fire and hunting could destroy the habitat they depended on. Assemblies formed around boundary markers, seasonal harvest calendars and mutual aid.  
**Economy:** Forest products, cultivated clearings, medicines, resin, woven fiber, river transport and carefully regulated Danger Beast materials.  
**Military:** Local scouts, hunters, defensive works, river patrols and occasional alliances with neighboring states. Their advantage is local terrain knowledge; their weakness is coordination across distant assemblies.  
**Law:** Resource rights vary by season and location. A tree, nesting ground or stream can have different permitted uses at different times. Outsiders may buy access, but a contract with one assembly does not grant rights throughout the forest.  
**Internal conflict:** Young merchants want wider export; conservation stewards warn that demand is outrunning regeneration. Some communities exploit outsiders while others oppose trade.  
**Information:** Oral routes, coded trail markers, messengers and biological observation networks. Rootsong users can gather limited local signals, not read every mind or monitor the entire forest.  
**Visual grammar:** Wet bark, jade canopy, red earth, elevated platforms, rain-shedding roofs, root-supported bridges where engineered, and clearings shaped around dry ground.  
**Failure conditions:** Drought, fire, invasive species, overhunting, logging concessions imposed by a state, or a council dispute that leaves shared routes unmaintained.  
**Player entry points:** Investigate a vanished animal population, negotiate a harvest boundary, protect a seed store, or discover that a supposedly sacred forest is being secretly logged.

## 7. The Aokiri Sound League — the politics of a narrow sea

**Government:** League of ports sharing pilotage rules, navigational markers, salvage law and limited naval obligations. Member ports remain economically competitive.  
**Core territory:** A strait between volcanic headlands and island chains.  
**Founding cause:** Shipwrecks and rival toll wars made reliable pilots and common signal conventions valuable.  
**Economy:** Pilotage, ship repair, fish, salt, sailcloth, navigation instruments, freight, salvage and harbor services.  
**Military:** Patrol vessels, harbor chains, shore batteries and pilots pressed into service. The league can deny a strait to small fleets but cannot defeat every major navy.  
**Law:** Salvage claims depend on proof of danger, rescue effort and prior ownership. False signal lights are treated as a grave crime because they can kill entire crews.  
**Internal conflict:** Ports dispute lighthouse funding, pilot certification and how much naval protection each member owes. Merchant houses want open passage; harbor authorities fear blockade.  
**Information:** Flags, lamps and sound signals are constrained by fog, weather, distance and line of sight. A ship can miss a signal without being incompetent.  
**Visual grammar:** Basalt, salt-white stone, storm teal, bronze patina, tide stairs, windbreak alleys and lighthouse silhouettes.  
**Failure conditions:** A failed breakwater, corrupted pilot registry, prolonged blockade, or a major port leaving the league.  
**Player entry points:** Escort a pilot, investigate a false beacon, recover a wreck record, or broker a truce between ports.

## 8. The Nine-Keel Directorate — a naval state built around industrial throughput

**Government:** A compact of admirals, shipwright guilds, timber stewards and dock authorities. Its formal council hides a constant struggle between military procurement and commercial repair.  
**Core territory:** A protected volcanic harbor with nearby timber islands and mineral uplands.  
**Economy:** Shipbuilding, rope, sailcloth, metal fittings, naval stores, repair contracts and convoy insurance. The state imports food and depends on managed forests for hull timber.  
**Military:** A professional fleet constrained by crew training, dock throughput, timber maturation, spare parts and maintenance. Ship count is not the same as ready fleet strength.  
**Law:** Timber concessions and dock labor are tightly regulated. Smuggling strategic materials is punished harshly, although powerful houses sometimes evade the rules.  
**Internal conflict:** Admirals demand warships; merchants demand cargo vessels; island communities resist quotas and deforestation.  
**Information:** Convoy reports arrive only when ships return or signal stations receive them. Admirals often make decisions from incomplete estimates of enemy fleet strength.  
**Visual grammar:** Dark basalt, lacquer red, salt-white dry docks, bronze fittings, crane silhouettes and smoke drifting downwind from foundries.  
**Failure conditions:** Timber shortage, dockyard strike, imported grain crisis, fleet disease or loss of a crucial dry dock.  
**Player entry points:** Investigate sabotage, protect a timber survey, negotiate a dock strike, or exploit the gap between official fleet counts and seaworthy ships.

## 9. The Ilyr — an umbrella term, not one elf kingdom

**Classification:** Original long-lived people, subject to further biological and linguistic design. “Ilyr” is a trade-language label. Communities have their own names and may reject the umbrella category.

### 9.1 Lifespan and demography
Long lifespan does not equal wisdom, perfect memory or moral superiority. If Ilyr mature slowly and live substantially longer than humans, their population growth, inheritance, political turnover, labor markets and attitudes toward risk must be designed accordingly. They can forget, misremember, suppress records, suffer cognitive decline, disagree over history and be deceived.

### 9.2 Three distinct communities

**The Elarin Watersheds:** Forest and river settlements organized around spring sources, canopy corridors and long-term stewardship agreements. Their disputes concern who may alter a watershed, not a universal hatred of technology.

**The Veyl Steps:** Highland communities with terraced agriculture, stone-root houses, weather observatories and strict winter stores. They are known for surveyors and masonry, but not every individual is a scholar or artisan.

**The Nhal Tunnels:** Subterranean Ilyr communities whose history is tied to karst systems, mineral rights and surface trade. They maintain ventilation, water and fungal cultivation networks; some are isolationist, others depend on surface partners.

### 9.3 Politics and diversity
- Some Ilyr city-leagues compete over water and trade.
- Some communities are monarchic, others governed by assemblies, religious councils or merchant houses.
- Some preserve old agreements; others exploit longevity to accumulate property and institutional power.
- Mixed settlements and families exist where contact is sustained; identity and legal status differ by region.
- Ilyr institutions are not automatically older than every human institution, and their records are not automatically more accurate.
- They have no innate universal telepathy, nature command or immunity to ordinary injury unless a specific original trait is defined and limited.

### 9.4 Visual language
Use local geology, forest type, climate and social organization. Avoid generic white marble, glowing trees and universal gold trim. Elarin architecture uses watershed markers and shaded water courts; Veyl buildings use stone terraces and windbreaks; Nhal sites emphasize airflow, sound cues and controlled lighting.

### 9.5 Relationship to the Empire
There is no automatic secret ancient war between all Ilyr and the Empire. Some communities may trade with Imperial ports, some may have little reliable knowledge of the Empire, and others may be involved in border diplomacy. Each relationship needs a date, route, source of information and political motive.

## 10. Beyond the planet: strict expansion gate

Interplanetary or otherworldly exploration remains possible, but it is not the default explanation for every mystery.

An off-world location requires:
- a defined physical or supernatural access mechanism;
- origin and destination;
- travel time, risk and resource cost;
- what can cross and what cannot;
- communication latency;
- why access is rare or constrained;
- political/ecological effects of contact;
- how this does not casually overwrite manga continuity.

The Empire incident remains its own manga continuity chain unless a player action creates a plausible bridge. The mere existence of off-world powers does not mean they know about, can reach, or have reason to interfere with Imperial events.
