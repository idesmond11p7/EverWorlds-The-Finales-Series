# 26. Physical World Skeleton v1: Continents, Climate Logic, Watersheds, and Route Constraints
Status: authored macro-geography proposal. This is an original setting framework, not Akame ga Kill manga canon. No city coordinates or final political borders are assigned here.

## 1. Purpose and hard constraints

This document advances the physical map from abstract generation rules into a usable macro-geography. It defines large land-and-water structures, climatic mechanisms, drainage logic, and route constraints. It does not pretend to be a surveyed atlas: exact coordinates, tectonic ages, distances, and local weather remain open until the map is rendered and checked.

Locked constraints:
- Total planetary surface area is 12.3 times Earth's surface area. This is an authorial constraint.
- Gravity is somewhat stronger than Earth's; its numerical value and consequences remain deferred.
- A large portion of the inhabited world is desert-dominated, but desert cultures must not collapse into one aesthetic, religion, or political system.
- The Empire is one enormous state on this planet, not the planet's only state and not the source of every culture or institution.
- Manga-canon Empire events retain their own uninterrupted continuity. Original macro-geography may surround that continuity but may not rewrite it.
- The names and macro-regions below are original planning labels unless explicitly marked otherwise. They are not claims about manga canon.

## 2. Planetary design model

Use a spherical planetary model for map logic even if the eventual map is presented as a stylized projection. Do not scale an Earth map by simply multiplying every linear distance by 12.3. The 12.3 multiplier applies to total surface area; a geometrically similar sphere would have a radius about sqrt(12.3), or 3.51 times Earth's radius. This creates major unresolved physical questions if Earth-like gravity and human-scale biology are assumed. The setting's slightly stronger gravity therefore cannot be derived from this radius without additional assumptions about density, composition, rotation, and the intended meaning of the scale constraint.

For practical world design, separate three layers:
1. **Authorial scale:** 12.3 Earth surface areas, locked.
2. **Map topology:** the relative arrangement of continents, seas, mountain systems, basins and chokepoints, authored here.
3. **Physical parameter model:** radius, density, gravity, rotation, axial tilt and orbital period, deliberately unresolved until the author chooses a consistent explanation.

Do not use a single global map scale to claim travel times until layer 3 and map distances are reconciled. Until then, distances and travel times should be expressed as provisional ranges and terrain-specific estimates.

## 3. Macro-geographic structure

The planet's land is organized into six major geographic systems separated by ocean basins and difficult crossings. These are design regions, not six equal continents.

### G-01 — The Imperial Heartland and Inner Marches
A broad temperate-to-subtropical land system containing the manga-era Empire's core and its surrounding contested marches. The Empire occupies a large portion of this system but does not own every coast, island, mountain refuge, or neighboring polity.

Physical structure:
- A central upland and broken mountain arc divide drainage into several river basins.
- Broad agricultural plains and older river corridors support dense settlement where soil and flood management permit.
- Northern highlands feed cold rivers and seasonal snowmelt systems.
- Western approaches include passes, dry interior corridors, and politically contested routes rather than a single universal frontier.
- Southern reaches transition through dry steppe and semi-arid basins toward the planet's wider desert system.

Design consequence: the Capital and Imperial institutions may project power along navigable rivers, maintained roads, canals, and military corridors, but the state's logistical reach degrades away from these arteries. Distance, terrain, weather, rebellion, and information delay must constrain authority.

### G-02 — The Great Arid Belt
A vast connected zone of hot deserts, cold deserts, semi-arid plateaus, salt basins, rocky uplands, dune seas, and seasonally flooded wadis. It is not one uninterrupted sand sea. Some regions are stone deserts, gravel plains, high cold basins, volcanic ashlands, or irrigated pockets.

Physical structure:
- Subtropical dry zones are reinforced by descending air and continental interiors far from reliable maritime moisture.
- Mountain rain shadows create adjacent wet windward slopes and dry leeward basins.
- Intermittent rivers flow after storms and may terminate in salt lakes or inland deltas.
- Deep aquifers vary in recharge age and quality. Fossil water cannot be treated as an infinite renewable resource.
- Oases occur where geology brings groundwater close to the surface, where rivers cross arid terrain, or where engineered capture makes water accessible.

Civilization consequence: settlements cluster around reliable water, trade nodes, defensible uplands, seasonal pasture, mining, or engineered water systems. Water rights, maintenance labor, contamination, and recharge are political forces. A desert route's viability depends on the spacing and seasonal reliability of water, not merely on whether a line can be drawn across the map.

### G-03 — The Monsoon Rim and Green Basins
A humid-to-seasonal region on the ocean-facing side of a major mountain chain. It includes wet forests, seasonal woodlands, floodplains, rice-like or grain-based agricultural basins, mangroves, and storm-exposed coasts, depending on latitude and elevation.

Physical structure:
- Moist maritime air rises along the coastal mountain wall, producing wet windward slopes.
- Rainfall decreases inland and across the range, creating a transition toward dry interior basins.
- Rivers may be strongly seasonal and flood-prone; broad floodplains require levees, elevated construction, flood calendars, or accepted seasonal loss.
- Warm coastal waters and seasonal wind reversal may support monsoon-like travel and agriculture, but exact direction and calendar remain provisional until axial tilt and rotation are fixed.

Civilization consequence: abundant biomass does not mean easy travel. Flooded roads, rot, insects, landslides, river crossings, and seasonal storms shape logistics and military campaigns. Architecture must respond to heat, moisture, drainage, and pests.

### G-04 — The Northern Shield and Cold Interior
A broad cold region of old uplands, boreal forest, tundra-like plains, glaciated ranges, lakes, and wind-scoured plateaus. It includes the northern territories relevant to Empire-adjacent geography, while the exact relation to manga-named places remains unassigned pending panel verification.

Physical structure:
- High latitude and elevation create long cold seasons and short growing seasons.
- Snowmelt and lake storage regulate major rivers.
- Permafrost or seasonally frozen ground can destabilize roads and foundations during thaw.
- Mountain passes may be closed for months; frozen rivers can temporarily become routes but are never automatically safe.
- Forest belts thin into open cold steppe and tundra according to moisture, soil and exposure.

Civilization consequence: population clusters around sheltered valleys, timber, fisheries, mineral deposits, navigable seasonal rivers and protected coasts. Winter changes travel and warfare rather than merely changing descriptive adjectives.

### G-05 — The Broken Western Lands
A fragmented system of peninsulas, inland seas, highlands, rift valleys, old volcanic provinces and narrow agricultural corridors. It supports many independent polities because ridges, straits and broken terrain make regional control difficult.

Physical structure:
- Multiple short watersheds descend from rugged interiors toward separate coasts or inland basins.
- Rift valleys may host lakes, geothermal areas and mineral deposits.
- Narrow passes and straits concentrate trade, tolls and military movement.
- Earthquake, volcanic and landslide hazards must be attached only where the geological model supports them, not distributed as generic spectacle.

Civilization consequence: a small state controlling a pass or harbor can matter disproportionately without being militarily powerful across open terrain. Languages, laws and building traditions can diverge across short distances because travel funnels through a few routes.

### G-06 — The Outer Maritime and Deepwater Systems
A wide oceanic system containing island arcs, shelf seas, storm belts, deep basins, reef zones, kelp or cold-water ecosystems, and multiple maritime cultures. Underwater societies and deepwater communities may exist as original setting elements, but they require plausible ecology, pressure adaptation or supernatural rules, food systems, energy, material supply, and communication constraints.

Physical structure:
- Continental shelves support ports, fisheries and coastal ecosystems.
- Island arcs may align with tectonic boundaries, with volcanic islands and deep trenches in the same broad region.
- Prevailing winds, currents, seasonal storms and ocean crossings determine reliable sailing routes.
- Reefs, shoals, straits and storm corridors create route choices and hazards.
- Deepwater settlement cannot be treated as a normal city placed underwater. Pressure, light, temperature, oxygen, food, waste, construction and access must be solved by species biology, engineering, or explicit supernatural systems.

Civilization consequence: oceanic distance can separate political and cultural systems as effectively as mountains. Maritime intelligence, ship repair, navigational knowledge and seasonal timing are strategic assets.

## 4. Relief and drainage rules

No river, lake, wetland or oasis is placed for visual convenience alone. Apply these checks in order:

1. Establish the mountain belts, high plateaus, volcanic uplands and major depressions.
2. Mark watershed divides and identify each basin's outlet or endorheic status.
3. Route rivers downhill, with tributaries joining the trunk in a direction consistent with elevation.
4. Add lakes where topography, glacial history, tectonic basins, volcanic dams or river processes can retain water.
5. Place deltas and wetlands where sediment and low gradients permit them.
6. Add seasonal streams and wadis based on rainfall and runoff, not as permanent blue lines in dry regions.
7. Place groundwater recharge areas, aquifer boundaries and discharge zones. Surface watersheds and aquifer boundaries need not coincide.
8. Place cities only after the water system exists; document withdrawals, upstream dependencies, contamination risks and downstream effects.

A river cannot casually cross a mountain divide. A canal can cross a divide only through an engineered route with a water source, locks or lift mechanisms where needed, maintenance, and a plausible energy/labor budget. A desert settlement cannot sustain a large population solely because its description calls it an oasis.

## 5. Climate and biome rules

Climate is driven by latitude, elevation, distance from the sea, prevailing circulation, ocean currents, mountain barriers, and seasonal changes. These are linked constraints, not independent random tags.

- **Windward mountain slopes:** generally wetter when moist air is forced upward.
- **Leeward basins:** often drier due to rain shadow, with severity depending on wind direction and mountain height.
- **Coastal moderation:** temperature ranges may be smaller near the sea, while fog, storms and humidity can rise.
- **Continental interiors:** often have larger seasonal temperature swings and reduced maritime moisture.
- **High elevations:** cooler and more exposed; tree line and snowline vary with latitude and local moisture.
- **Cold currents:** can stabilize coastal air and contribute to fog or aridity in suitable circulation patterns.
- **Warm currents:** can increase coastal moisture and storm energy where winds carry that moisture inland.
- **Floodplains:** fertile but exposed to inundation, sediment shifts and disease ecology.
- **Desert margins:** often ecologically productive but vulnerable to grazing pressure, drought and shifting rainfall.
- **Wet tropical or monsoon zones:** face flood, fungal decay, insect pressure and difficult overland movement.

Do not make biome borders perfectly smooth. Use ecotones, elevation bands, seasonal wetlands, dry valleys, and local anomalies, but each anomaly must have a physical, ecological, historical or explicitly supernatural cause.

## 6. Route graph and movement costs

Represent travel as a graph:
- **Nodes:** settlements, ports, wells, passes, ferries, bridges, tunnel mouths, caravan stations, border posts and known hazards.
- **Edges:** roads, trails, rivers, sea lanes, passes, seasonal ice routes and maintained tunnels.
- **Edge attributes:** length range, terrain, elevation gain, seasonal availability, expected travel time, water/food intervals, carrying capacity, tolls, patrol presence, communication delay and known failure modes.

A route is not just a line between two places. A route can be physically possible but economically irrational, politically closed, seasonally dangerous or too water-poor for the current party.

Travel estimates must account for:
- travel mode and load;
- terrain and slope;
- weather, daylight and season;
- rest, injury, fatigue and food;
- animal or vehicle limits;
- road quality and maintenance;
- checkpoints, tolls, conflict and detours;
- uncertainty in local knowledge.

Never generate an exact travel time from an unverified distance. Use a range and state the dominant uncertainty. Travel times should be recalculated when a road collapses, a bridge is damaged, a pass closes, a war begins, or a water source fails.

## 7. Settlement placement tests

Before establishing a major settlement, record:
- reliable water source and seasonal failure risk;
- food hinterland, imports and storage;
- building materials and energy;
- flood, storm, fire, earthquake, landslide, drought or cold exposure as applicable;
- route access and the chokepoints controlling it;
- waste disposal and contamination pathway;
- productive basis and export/import balance;
- labor and demographic support;
- political authority, jurisdiction and local autonomy;
- dependence on upstream infrastructure or distant supply;
- reason the settlement persists despite its costs.

For a major city, one sentence saying “trade hub” is insufficient. Identify what is traded, why the route passes there, who maintains it, what alternatives exist, what can interrupt it, and who benefits from controlling the node.

## 8. Original place atlas placement protocol

The 60 current atlas entries are place seeds, not yet a fully mapped settlement layer. Assign them to macro-regions only after the first terrain and watershed sketch. For each placement:
1. Choose a physically compatible landform or coast.
2. Connect it to a valid watershed, aquifer, coast, pass or resource.
3. Define at least two plausible access routes where geography permits; identify the single chokepoint if there is only one.
4. Record the seasonal route changes.
5. Identify the nearest settlements and their trade, political or ecological dependencies.
6. Check whether another atlas entry already fills the same geographic/economic niche. If so, differentiate it or move it.
7. Leave exact coordinates provisional until a scaled map exists.

The 12 desert civilization concepts from document 16 should be distributed across different desert types and water systems, not clustered as interchangeable sand cities. The two 30-place atlas batches must likewise be checked for biome and economic redundancy before placement.

## 9. Empire integration boundary

The manga-era Empire is anchored to its canon geography only to the extent directly supported by the manga and verified references. Do not quietly assign original continents, exact coordinates, or borders to canon locations without evidence.

Original design can establish neighboring states, distant regions, maritime routes, and independent institutions outside the Empire's established footprint. It must not retroactively turn every original culture into an Imperial province or make the Empire the cause of all supernatural traditions.

The Empire's logistical power should be modeled through actual corridors: roads, rivers, canals, passes, ports, depots, garrisons, tax points and courier networks. Its legal claim over a territory is not the same as effective control of every village, road or mountain valley.

## 10. Open decisions and validation queue

Unresolved and not to be silently guessed:
- planetary radius, density, gravity value and reconciliation with the locked surface-area multiplier;
- rotation period, axial tilt, orbital eccentricity and seasonal calendar;
- land/ocean fraction and final continent silhouettes;
- latitude bands and exact global wind/currents;
- tectonic plate layout, mountain ages and active geological hazards;
- map projection and scale;
- exact distances, coordinates, travel times and border lines;
- which original atlas places share watersheds, routes or regional markets;
- direct manga-panel verification of named canon geography.

## 11. Validation gates before location coordinates

A macro-map draft passes only if:
- every major river has a plausible source, downhill path and outlet or endorheic basin;
- every desert has an explained moisture deficit or rain-shadow mechanism;
- mountain ranges create coherent windward/leeward patterns;
- major settlements have water, food, materials, routes and a reason to persist;
- maritime routes respect coasts, currents, storms and navigable passages;
- the Great Arid Belt contains multiple desert types and distinct water regimes;
- no city is assigned a final coordinate before the physical geography exists;
- the Empire remains a major state rather than the planet's universal center;
- canon geography claims are separated from original setting decisions;
- unresolved physical parameters remain labeled instead of being converted into fake precision.

## 12. Next dependency

Next required artifact: a schematic map specification that can be drawn and reviewed before coordinates are committed. It should define continent silhouettes, approximate latitude bands, tectonic and mountain axes, ocean basins, watershed divides, major river systems, rain-shadow zones and route chokepoints. After that, place the 60 atlas seeds and 12 desert civilizations against the physical map, then test travel and resource dependencies. Do not jump straight to district street maps while planetary hydrology is still undefined.
