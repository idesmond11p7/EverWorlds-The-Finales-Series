## 0. The principle

**Do not write a soundscape as background decoration. Write it as the audible behavior of a place Ira is moving through.**

The target combines three related but distinct effects:

1. **Acoustic presence:** sounds seem to occupy a physical environment, with sources, distances, barriers, reflections, masking, and changes as Ira moves.
2. **Gameworld density:** the environment suggests more life and activity than Ira is directly looking at. A door, shopfront, stairwell, passing vehicle, or half-heard exchange implies people pursuing ordinary purposes outside his immediate scene.
3. **Playable-feeling traversal in prose:** the reader can mentally track where Ira is, what he can reach or choose, what blocks him, what changes if he moves, and what new information becomes available. This is not a literal game interface or a sequence of quest markers.

The desired feeling is **not** “the narrator describes every detail in RTX quality.” It is that the reader's mental image updates as Ira's position, attention, and understanding change. LIT-17 remains the underlying perceptual engine; LIT-18 emphasizes sound as spatial evidence and the city as an active, partly unseen system.

## 1. Research basis: what can safely be transferred

### 1.1 Sound reveals space, not just mood

Game-audio research treats sound as a way to establish orientation, spatial scale, enclosure, environmental identity, and emotional tone. A sound can change as the player moves, enters a room, passes an obstacle, or leaves a source behind. Sound therefore helps a person infer the structure of a space and their relation to it. The same principle can be translated into prose without imitating game audio literally.

- *Architectonics of Videogame Sound Design: The Use of Sound and Music in Creating Immersive Virtual Spaces* (2025) discusses sound as both atmospheric and functional spatial information, including changing sound fields during movement and transitions. https://doi.org/10.1080/20507828.2025.2530892
- Grimshaw, *Sound and Player Immersion in Digital Games* (2012), considers sound, image, player position, and immersion together. https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780195388947.013.0084
- Zolin, *Unbound Acoustics: Embracing Complexity in Virtual Soundscapes* (2024), treats sound as part of the relationship among player, avatar, material environment, and action. https://doi.org/10.60923/issn.2724-2463/21426

**Transfer rule:** every important sound should have a plausible source and spatial relationship. Its prose function may be orientation, interruption, social evidence, atmosphere, or a change in Ira's attention. Do not make all sounds do all jobs.

### 1.2 Places can tell stories without explaining themselves

Environmental-storytelling research argues that players infer histories and situations from arrangements of objects, spaces, traces, and affordances. A 2025 CHI study by Smith Nicholls and Cook found that players interpreted a ruined game environment through a developing mental model built from material evidence and affordances. A 2026 DiGRA study of *What Remains of Edith Finch* describes environment as perceptual cues, action affordances, affective tone, and spatial metaphor.

- Smith Nicholls & Cook (2025), *Archaeological Gameworld Affordances*. https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.371403
- Xiang (2026), *Embodied Engagement with Environmental Storytelling in Walking Simulators: A Case Study of What Remains of Edith Finch*. https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/embodied-engagement-with-environmental-storytelling-in-walking-si/

**Transfer rule:** the reader may infer that a building is inhabited, that someone is waiting, that a business is closing, or that a conversation is private from partial evidence. But the evidence must support the inference, and Ira can be wrong about its meaning. Ambiguity should concern interpretation, not basic physical layout.

### 1.3 The Arkham lesson: responsive sound and life beyond the camera

A production interview about *Batman: Arkham City* describes dialogue and city sound changing with distance, reflections, and approach, helping Gotham feel populated while Batman moves through it. A sound-design account for *Batman: Arkham Shadow* describes ordinary lives leaking through apartment doors—arguments, crying babies, and private domestic activity—so the city feels inhabited even when those residents are not in the foreground.

- *AudioTechnology*, “Batman: Arkham City.” https://www.audiotechnology.com/features/batman-arkham-city
- Unlock Audio, “Creating the Sounds of Gotham in Batman: Arkham Shadow.” https://www.unlockaudio.com/blog/creating-the-sounds-of-gotham-in-batman-arkham-shadow/

These are production accounts, not controlled experiments proving a universal immersion formula. Their value here is concrete craft evidence: environmental sound can imply distance, movement, population, and private lives without forcing the protagonist to meet every person.

**Transfer rule:** let Kuoh continue beyond Ira's attention. A partly heard conversation, a shutter closing down the street, a television behind a thin wall, or a person reacting to an event Ira did not see can suggest a larger living system. Use only details that fit the exact location, time, and material culture.

## 2. The sound-to-prose pipeline

For any acoustically meaningful beat, privately resolve:

**source → action → path → listener position → audible change → attention/action consequence**

Example: a shop door opens behind Ira. The bell above it rings; street noise briefly enters the café; a customer speaks from near the doorway; the counter and other voices partly mask the words. Ira turns only if the sound matters to his immediate goal. Do not write a generic paragraph listing the bell, traffic, cups, footsteps, voices, and ventilation just because they exist.

Use a few distinguishable layers:

- **Foreground:** the sound that currently matters (a voice, cup, door, footstep).
- **Middle distance:** activity that locates other people or work in the space.
- **Environmental bed:** traffic, rain, rail noise, ventilation, or other persistent context.
- **Change or interruption:** a door opens, a vehicle masks speech, a shutter stops, a nearby voice falls silent.
- **Attention shift:** Ira notices, ignores, misattributes, or reinterprets a sound for a reason.

These are options, not a mandatory five-item checklist. Often one foreground sound and one changed background layer are enough.

### Sound is spatial evidence

- **Distance:** words lose clarity or blend into general speech; do not claim Ira can hear exact distant dialogue without a reason.
- **Obstruction:** a wall, closed door, partition, passing vehicle, or crowd can reduce or interrupt a source.
- **Enclosure:** hard, enclosed spaces may make sounds linger or reflect more than open spaces, but do not invent elaborate acoustic physics.
- **Movement:** a source grows clearer, shifts direction, becomes blocked, or fades as Ira moves.
- **Masking:** a louder event may cover a quieter one. Ira may catch only part of a sentence.
- **Silence:** silence is not an absence of all sound. It may be a local gap created when one source stops, making another source noticeable.

Use plain, character-accessible language. Do not use technical sound terminology in Ira's narration unless he plausibly knows it.

## 3. “Game prose” without game UI

The useful quality in game exploration is **continuous cause and consequence**, not gamification.

When Ira traverses a place, preserve:

1. **Orientation:** where he is relative to useful landmarks, exits, people, and obstacles.
2. **Affordances:** what he can realistically do from here—enter, wait, ask, look, pass, turn back, or spend money.
3. **Constraints:** what limits the choice—closed doors, distance, darkness, embarrassment, cash, fatigue, unfamiliar streets, a dead phone, or social expectations.
4. **Feedback:** what changes after he acts. A door opens; someone answers; the route is blocked; a sound becomes clearer; a person misunderstands him.
5. **Updated model:** what Ira now thinks is possible, and what he still does not know.

The environment should not merely wait for Ira to activate it. Shopkeepers close up because it is evening; customers leave because they have somewhere to be; a bus continues its route; someone checks the time; another person ignores him because their own problem matters more. These are not random decorative events. They are traces of independent purposes.

### Avoid false simulation

Do not manufacture activity by making every passer-by do something conspicuously meaningful. Real places contain routine, repetition, waiting, mundane inefficiency, and activity irrelevant to the protagonist. The point is not maximum event density; it is the sense that events arise from a world with more causes than Ira can see.

Do not make every detail a clue. A strange behavior can remain unexplained. A sound can be ordinary. A person can enter, buy something, and leave without becoming a plot device.

## 4. Visual “RTX” rendering: coherent, not maximal

The user's RTX shorthand means physically legible, materially responsive visual experience—not simply more adjectives, cinematic slow motion, or a permanent ultra-detailed camera.

Select visual details through the same causal model as sound:

- What light source illuminates the surface?
- What is between Ira and the object?
- What changes when he moves or turns?
- Which surfaces reflect, absorb, obscure, or scatter light in a plausible way?
- What can Ira actually see from this position?
- Does the detail affect his action, interpretation, or emotional state?

Use one or two connected details that materially change the mental image. For example, a shop window may reflect the street over the dim interior, so Ira first sees his own outline and moving headlights, then shifts sideways and makes out the counter behind the glass. The visual description also establishes geometry and his movement. Do not describe every reflection, texture, light source, and color in the street.

**First-image rule:** the first spatial image must not imply the wrong thing. A train should read as a train, not a cable car; a doorway should have a clear relation to the counter; a sound behind a wall should not be treated as if the speaker stood beside Ira. Correct the spatial model before polishing the sentence.

## 5. Point of audition and point of view

The soundscape belongs to Ira's situated access, not to an omniscient audio mixer. He cannot hear every room at once. His current goal, uncertainty, curiosity, embarrassment, fatigue, and orientation determine which sounds rise into attention.

A sound may be present without being noticed. If Ira notices it later, give the reader a reason: another sound stopped, he moved closer, a speaker repeated it, or it became relevant to what he was doing. Do not retroactively pretend he heard information he could not have identified at the time.

In first-person prose, the narrator may describe the sound plainly and let Ira's reaction carry its meaning. Do not attach a psychological verdict to every acoustic event. A laugh from another table is evidence of a laugh; whether it concerns Ira remains unknown unless additional cues support the conclusion.

## 6. Chapter 8 application

Chapter 8's evening should not be rendered as a horror soundscape from beginning to end. It needs contrast.

- **Apartment:** the kettle, envelope, placard, room, and street beyond the window create a bounded sound field. The quiet makes his thoughts harder to avoid; it does not require ominous silence or supernatural noises.
- **Walk:** the sound field opens and changes with distance and movement. Give the reader a few recognizable sources rather than “the city hummed.”
- **Kissaten:** foreground speech and small actions establish social distance; background customers and street noise imply other lives. A door or passing vehicle may interrupt a conversation.
- **Awkward exchange:** let sound shape timing—he misses a word, answers after a beat, hears the small laugh, or notices the other teenagers have already returned to their own conversation. Do not inflate the embarrassment with exaggerated heartbeats and room-wide silence.
- **Return and decision:** ordinary room sounds and the dossier reconnect the outing to the practical problem. The next undertaking comes from what Ira has learned and cannot yet explain, not from an ominous audio cue.

Do not give every sound an emotional label. Let the physical event arrive first; allow Ira's interpretation to be partial.

## 7. Failure modes

Reject a passage if it relies on any of these shortcuts:

- **Acoustic wallpaper:** every street has the same hum, footsteps, chatter, and distant traffic.
- **Sound inventory:** unrelated noises are listed to prove immersion.
- **Impossible hearing:** Ira understands distant or obstructed speech without sufficient access.
- **Omniscient ambience:** the narration reports sounds from locations Ira cannot hear.
- **Clue inflation:** every odd noise or behavior hints at a conspiracy or supernatural event.
- **Game UI leakage:** quest notifications, objective markers, status messages, or an artificial mission-giver replace causal story development.
- **Decorative RTX:** every surface gets elaborate reflection and texture language without changing the reader's mental image.
- **Static city:** people and businesses exist only to react to Ira.
- **Over-scripted city:** every stranger performs a meaningful beat, making the world feel like a theme park built around the protagonist.
- **False geometry:** the prose makes a reader imagine the wrong vehicle, room layout, distance, barrier, or direction.
- **Emotional overmixing:** every ordinary sound is made ominous, every pause is profound, or every social cue becomes a dramatic event.

## 8. Verification test

For each major sound-rich or traversal passage, ask:

1. Can the reader identify the source of the important sound, or is uncertainty deliberately preserved?
2. Is its distance/obstruction/movement compatible with Ira's position?
3. Does the soundscape change because Ira or something else changes position or activity?
4. Is the detail selected by attention rather than included as a checklist?
5. Does it help orientation, action, inference, atmosphere, or a change in attention?
6. Can the city be imagined continuing beyond the immediate scene without turning every detail into a clue?
7. Does the environment respond to action through ordinary causes?
8. Is Ira's knowledge bounded by what he can perceive and reasonably infer?
9. Does the prose preserve the intended first mental image and physical geometry?
10. Could a sentence be removed without losing spatial, causal, emotional, or world information? If yes, cut or repurpose it.

**Acceptance condition:** a reader should be able to mentally track where Ira is, hear a few distinct sources as belonging to that place, understand how his movement changes access to them, and sense that other lives continue outside his focus—without feeling that the narrator is demonstrating sound design or that the story has become a videogame interface.
