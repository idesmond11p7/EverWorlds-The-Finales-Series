# EverWorlds — Narrative Prose Architecture & Reading Surface Standard

Status: ACTIVE PRODUCTION STANDARD
Version: 1.0
Scope: EverWorlds: The Finales Series
Applies to: chapter prose, scene prose, environmental description, paragraphing, sentence cadence, and digital reading presentation

## 0. Core problem this standard solves

The target is not merely prose that is easy to understand.

The target is prose that causes the reader to construct and continuously update a convincing situation model: space, objects, movement, character attention, causality, time, atmosphere, and emotional interpretation.

Current failure mode:
- observation-by-observation narration
- excessive paragraph fragmentation
- sentences functioning as isolated captions
- environmental details named without enough spatial relationship
- readability optimized at the expense of perceptual accumulation
- paragraph breaks used as generic whitespace instead of meaningful discourse boundaries
- sentence rhythm made uniformly easy, producing a narrated/listed feeling
- visual page texture too sparse to carry scene mass

Target:
- clear sentences
- coherent perceptual units
- paragraphs with internal accumulation
- deliberate paragraph boundaries
- variable sentence cadence
- spatial relationships rather than object inventories
- physical details that constrain the reader's mental rendering
- typography that supports immersion rather than competing with it

## 1. Research basis

### 1.1 Situation models are the cognitive target

Narrative readers construct integrated mental representations of described situations rather than merely retaining a sequence of words. Situation models incorporate dimensions including location, objects, characters, actions, time, causality, and intentionality.

Bower & Morrow (1990) found that readers construct mental models of story situations and characters, with attention to protagonist movement activating nearby parts of the represented environment.

Zwaan & Radvansky's review (1998) treats situation models as integrated representations of described states of affairs and emphasizes their multidimensional nature.

Spatial situation-model research shows that readers track spatial relations and that accessibility is shaped by the protagonist's current location and spatial proximity.

Production consequence:
> Description must give the reader enough relational information to maintain a world, not merely enough nouns to recognize objects.

### 1.2 Readers update models at meaningful changes

Bailey et al. (2017) found that readers segment narratives into events and that changes in character or spatial location can function as event boundaries; attentional orientation affects how readers update situation models.

Pettijohn & Radvansky (2016) found increased reading times around narrative event boundaries, consistent with readers updating situation models when events change.

Eye-movement research likewise finds that event structure predicts reading behavior and that event boundaries carry measurable processing effects.

Production consequence:
> A paragraph break should normally correspond to a meaningful change in perceptual, spatial, temporal, causal, or attentional organization—not simply a desire to make text look airy.

This does NOT mean every paragraph must represent a new event. Paragraphs may subdivide one event when attention or discourse function changes.

### 1.3 Paragraphs are psychologically meaningful discourse units

Stark (1988) found that paragraph boundaries influenced which ideas readers judged important even when paragraphing did not simply change reading speed.

Bond & Hayes (1984) found that readers can consistently restore paragraph boundaries when those markers are removed. Readers rely heavily on cohesion breaks, topic shifts, and paragraph length when deciding where paragraphs belong.

Kieras (1978) found that poorly organized paragraph structure increased processing demands and reduced gist recall in simple passages.

Production consequence:
> Paragraphing is not decoration. It changes emphasis, grouping, and how information is integrated.

Therefore:
- Do not split paragraphs mechanically.
- Do not merge paragraphs merely to make them look denser.
- Place boundaries where the discourse has completed a coherent unit or attention has genuinely shifted.

## 2. Paragraph architecture

### 2.1 The fundamental unit

For EverWorlds:

> Sentence = local perceptual/semantic movement.
> Paragraph = coherent perceptual/discourse unit.
> Scene = sequence of connected situation-model states.
> Chapter = sequence of larger narrative states.

A paragraph should leave the reader holding a relationship, image, action, inference, or emotional state—not a list of disconnected facts.

### 2.2 Paragraph accumulation rule

Related information should accumulate inside a paragraph until its perceptual or discourse function is complete.

Weak pattern:
- building
- pavement
- shutter
- smell
- wires
- traffic

Stronger pattern:
- establish observer position
- establish surrounding space
- place major structures
- connect objects through activity
- introduce secondary sensory information
- let the character's attention settle or move
- then break

The paragraph should behave like a camera/attention movement without literally becoming camera prose.

### 2.3 Paragraph boundary triggers

Strong reasons to break:
1. protagonist changes location
2. meaningful spatial orientation changes
3. new event begins
4. action phase changes
5. character interaction begins/ends
6. sensory focus shifts substantially
7. internal thought interrupts external perception
8. important realization lands
9. time advances
10. narrative emphasis deliberately contracts
11. a sentence needs isolated visual/emotional weight

Weak reasons to break:
- paragraph has reached an arbitrary sentence count
- every observation deserves its own line
- a short sentence “looks good” alone
- generic readability anxiety
- trying to imitate chat formatting

### 2.4 Paragraph length

No fixed sentence count.

Use variable lengths according to function:
- 1 sentence: impact, realization, isolated perception, deliberate compression
- 2–4 sentences: focused action or narrow observation
- 4–8+ sentences: environmental sweep, continuous action, spatial construction, reflective movement
- longer blocks are acceptable when the situation genuinely remains continuous

Do not force all paragraphs into the same visual size.

## 3. Sentence architecture

### 3.1 Sentence rhythm is perceptual control

Research on punctuation and eye movements indicates that punctuation creates processing/pausing effects. Research on clause and discourse ordering also shows that information organization affects reading behavior.

Therefore sentence length and syntax should correspond to attention.

Longer/compound sentences can:
- connect multiple environmental relations
- carry movement through space
- show continuous action
- preserve causal/temporal continuity
- allow several details to be integrated into one perceptual unit

Short sentences can:
- stop attention
- register a realization
- mark a physical change
- isolate an object
- create uncertainty
- accelerate action
- create aftermath

Fragments should be rare and intentional.

### 3.2 Avoid uniform cadence

Do not produce a chapter where nearly every sentence is:
subject + verb + short object.

That produces a low-information rhythm even when the vocabulary is good.

Target cadence:
- flowing sentence
- flowing sentence
- shorter sentence
- medium sentence
- longer spatial sentence
- paragraph closure

The pattern must vary naturally rather than becoming another formula.

## 4. Spatial rendering

### 4.1 Objects need relationships

Do not merely enumerate:
> road, shop, van, tree, building, wire.

Instead establish:
- where the protagonist is
- what is closest
- what is behind/beyond/above
- what obstructs sight
- what moves
- what remains fixed
- what is partially visible
- what sound arrives from outside the visual field

Readers track spatial situation models through relations, not isolated labels.

### 4.2 POV is the rendering camera

Description must be constrained by the protagonist's actual attention.

Ira does not receive an omniscient environmental scan.

He notices:
- what catches attention
- what is useful
- what moves
- what is unfamiliar
- what he has reason to inspect
- what he overlooks
- what he remembers

The environment continues independently of him, but the prose only renders the portion his perception can access.

### 4.3 Environmental depth

A convincing scene should usually contain some combination of:
- foreground
- middle distance
- background
- occlusion
- independent motion
- material surfaces
- sound source/location
- atmospheric condition

Not every paragraph needs all of them.

## 5. Concrete rendering over abstract visual labels

Do not rely on:
- beautiful
- cinematic
- magical
- dark
- vibrant
- lively
- gloomy
- peaceful

as substitutes for physical rendering.

Abstract interpretation may appear, but physical conditions should establish the image first.

Instead of:
> The street was beautiful.

Prefer details that constrain rendering:
- illumination source
- surface response
- weather
- distance
- material
- movement
- colour relationships
- sound

Important:
> Beautiful does not mean bright.
> Dark does not mean night.
> Realistic does not mean desaturated.
> Detailed does not mean adjective-heavy.

## 6. Digital typography / text graphics

Typography has two separate jobs:
1. maintain readable access to the prose
2. provide a visual rhythm compatible with the narrative

Research on digital typography shows that font, spacing, line width, and other visual variables can measurably affect eye movements and reading performance.

Scaltritti et al. (2019) found significant effects of typographic variables on eye movements and reading performance across readers.

Minakata & Beier (2021) found font width changed eye-movement patterns even when reading speed was maintained.

Wallace et al. (2022) found substantial individual differences in reading speed across fonts, supporting the idea that there is no universally optimal font for every reader.

Rello et al. (2016) found font size affected readability and comprehension in a web-reading experiment, while extreme line-spacing conditions could impair readability.

W3C guidance separately recommends constrained line width and adequate spacing as accessibility mechanisms; these are presentation guidelines, not novel prose rules.

Production consequence:
> We should control the text surface enough that it supports reading, but we should not mistake accessibility spacing rules for literary paragraph architecture.

For Scribble Hub/web presentation:
- left-aligned text
- comfortable line length
- readable body size
- stable line spacing
- consistent paragraph separation
- no justified text
- no decorative formatting during ordinary narration
- avoid giant walls of text when a real discourse boundary exists
- avoid artificially tiny “caption” paragraphs throughout a chapter

## 7. Typography as narrative instrument

Ordinary prose should make typography nearly invisible.

Typography becomes noticeable only when the story has a reason to disrupt normal reading:
- reality instability
- fragmented perception
- messages/screens
- documents
- system-like material if canon requires it
- extreme psychological rupture

Do not use:
- random bolding
- excessive italics
- one-sentence paragraphs everywhere
- artificial line breaks
- “poetry” formatting for ordinary prose
- decorative symbols as a substitute for prose control

The page should feel like a novel first.

## 8. Information density

The target is not maximum description.

The target is maximum useful relational information per perceptual unit.

A paragraph should answer some combination of:
- Where am I?
- What is around me?
- What is moving?
- What is changing?
- What matters to the protagonist?
- What can the protagonist infer?
- What remains uncertain?

Avoid adding details solely because they are realistic.

Realism is selective.

A real person does not consciously inventory every pipe, window, vehicle, tree, and wall.

## 9. Scene-level accumulation

A scene should become more concrete as the reader remains in it.

First contact:
- broad orientation

Continued presence:
- specific spatial relationships

Closer attention:
- material/sensory detail

Interaction:
- objects gain causal relevance

Departure:
- selected details remain as memory anchors

This creates the feeling that the reader has occupied a place rather than received a description of it.

## 10. Chapter 6 application

Chapter 6 currently fails this standard in several places.

Primary problems:
- too many one- and two-sentence paragraphs
- repeated “ordinary/normal” conclusions
- several environmental lists are split into separate perceptual fragments
- travel segments sometimes enumerate rather than spatially compose
- emotional interpretation is occasionally stated immediately after physical description instead of being allowed to emerge
- some isolated sentences have no structural reason to be isolated
- the chapter is readable but visually and cognitively too lightweight for the intended EverWorlds rendering target

Required revision method:
1. preserve canon and scene order
2. preserve actual plot events
3. consolidate related environmental details
4. rebuild paragraph boundaries around perceptual/discourse units
5. vary sentence architecture
6. preserve deliberate short lines where they create actual emphasis
7. remove redundant “ordinary” commentary where the physical scene already communicates it
8. strengthen spatial relationships rather than merely adding adjectives
9. preserve Ira's POV and knowledge limits
10. perform a final page-shape audit after prose revision

## 11. QC tests

Before a chapter is marked LOCKED, test:

### Paragraph test
Can each paragraph be named by its dominant perceptual/discourse function?

### Boundary test
If the paragraph break moved one sentence earlier/later, would the grouping become less coherent?

### Image test
After reading a paragraph, can a reader retain a spatial or sensory relationship rather than just a list of nouns?

### Cadence test
Are long and short sentences doing different jobs?

### Attention test
Does the prose follow Ira's attention rather than an omniscient camera?

### Density test
Are related observations accumulating before the prose breaks?

### Page test
Does the chapter visually resemble continuous novel prose rather than a transcript/chat/script?

### Abstraction test
Could an abstract adjective be replaced by physical conditions?

### Repetition test
Are words such as “normal,” “ordinary,” “strange,” “different,” and “yesterday” carrying interpretation that the scene itself could communicate?

### Continuity test
Does the rewrite preserve locked canon and chapter ownership?

## 12. Non-negotiable style identity

EverWorlds prose should be:

Grounded.
Spatial.
Material.
Perceptual.
Variable.
Readable.
Dense when the world needs to exist.
Sparse when attention needs to contract.
Concrete before interpretive.
Cinematic in spatial consequence, not in adjective count.
Lyrical only when perception itself becomes lyrical.

The governing principle:

> Do not make the reader understand a world.
> Make the reader continuously construct one.
