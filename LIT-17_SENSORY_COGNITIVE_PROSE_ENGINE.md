## 0. Purpose

This document defines a repeatable method for making prose feel physically inhabited and psychologically immediate without turning description into an inventory. It is not a list of decorative techniques. It connects:
**human perception → attention and interpretation → action/feedback → reader situation model → sentence/paragraph decisions.**

The objective is not maximal sensory detail. It is *high-value, correctly selected sensory information* that lets readers reconstruct the scene, experience the character's immediate concerns, and infer more than the narration explains.

This is a working engine, not a claim that neuroscience can dictate literary taste. Research constrains implausible claims and informs technique selection; manuscript experiments decide whether a technique actually works in this voice.

## 1. Authority and evidence rules

Every claim in downstream documents must be classified as one of:
- **CANON:** author-locked fact or verified manuscript fact.
- **SOURCE:** a finding or argument stated by an identifiable research source.
- **SYNTHESIS:** a practical rule inferred by combining sources and narrative requirements.
- **HYPOTHESIS:** a technique expected to work but not yet tested in the manuscript.
- **PROPOSAL:** an optional story/scene choice awaiting approval.
- **UNKNOWN:** unresolved; do not fill it with a plausible guess.

Research does not authorize plot invention. Canon does not automatically mean the mechanism is fully understood. A research-informed technique must not be described as experimentally proven to cause a specific literary effect unless the evidence actually tests that effect.

## 2. The perceptual pipeline

Use this as the internal model for building a scene. It is not a checklist to expose to readers.

1. **Available world:** many objects, people, movements, surfaces, and sound sources exist simultaneously.
2. **Sensory access:** Ira receives only information physically available from his position, body, orientation, attention, and environmental conditions.
3. **Selection:** goals, novelty, learned expectations, threat sensitivity, fatigue, interest, and current thought make some signals more salient than others.
4. **Grouping:** separate signals become perceived sources or events: a voice behind a door, traffic beyond the shopfront, footsteps approaching from the same pavement.
5. **Interpretation:** Ira assigns a tentative cause or meaning, sometimes correctly and sometimes incorrectly.
6. **Body response:** orientation, pace, posture, grip, breath, muscle tension, temperature sensation, or movement changes when the situation warrants it.
7. **Action:** he approaches, waits, withdraws, speaks, misreads, ignores, or tests something.
8. **Feedback:** the environment or another person responds. Ira may update his model—or protect his first interpretation.
9. **Reader update:** the prose gives the reader enough evidence to construct a coherent situation and make their own inferences.

**Core rule:** do not skip from stimulus directly to a polished psychological explanation when the intervening perception, interpretation, or action is the interesting part.

## 3. Research foundations and what they do—and do not—establish

### 3.1 Situation models and embodied comprehension
Zwaan and Radvansky's review describes situation models as integrated mental representations of described states of affairs, not merely memories of wording. Later work reviews grounded cognition and mental simulation in discourse comprehension. This supports treating spatial relations, action, time, causality, and sensory consequences as mutually informing parts of the reader's model rather than independent decorative details.
Sources:
- Zwaan & Radvansky (1998), *Situation Models in Language Comprehension and Memory*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9522683/
- Zwaan (2016), *Situation Models, Mental Simulations, and Abstract Concepts in Discourse Comprehension*: https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-015-0864-x
- Zwaan (2025), *From Words to Worlds: Twenty-Five Years of Advances in Situation Model Research*: https://doi.org/10.1177/09637214251326812

**Operational synthesis:** each important description should improve at least one of the reader's models: where Ira is, what is happening, what can be acted upon, what something may mean, or how his interpretation changes.

### 3.2 Auditory scene analysis
Human hearing must separate a mixture of overlapping signals into perceptually meaningful sources and streams. Attention and task goals influence which sounds are tracked. Soundscape appraisal also depends on context and interpretation, not acoustic properties alone.
Sources:
- Gutschalk & Dykstra (2014), *Functional Imaging of Auditory Scene Analysis*: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heares.2013.11.005
- Shinn-Cunningham and colleagues, *Auditory Scene Analysis: An Attention Perspective*: https://pubs.asha.org/doi/abs/10.1044/2017_JSLHR-H-17-0041
- *Human Auditory Ecology* (2023): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10658775/
- *Appraising the Sonic Environment* (2025): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12189148/
- *Soundscape and its context: A framework based on a systematic review*: https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0036882

**Operational synthesis:** never render a city as one undifferentiated “noise.” Build a few concurrent, separable sound sources whose relation to space can be inferred. A sound's narrative value depends on its source, distance, obstruction, rhythm, persistence, and what Ira does with it.

### 3.3 Point of view and represented thought
Free indirect discourse can carry a character's evaluative language, emotional colouring, and judgments within the narration without constant “I thought” tags. It is not automatically more immersive in every context; its effect depends on surrounding prose and voice. Research comparing narrative modes suggests first-person narration can support identification and spatial perspective-taking, but no POV format guarantees reader identification.
Sources:
- Gunn, *Free Indirect Discourse*, Oxford Research Encyclopedia: https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780190201098.013.1020
- Bray, *The ‘dual voice’ of free indirect discourse: a reading experiment*: https://doi.org/10.1177/0963947007072844
- *Does narrative perspective influence readers’ perspective-taking?*: https://doi.org/10.5334/gjgl.225

**Operational synthesis:** use first-person immediacy as the default for Ira, but don't mechanically report every act of sensing. Let his vocabulary and judgments shape the sentence. Keep his interpretation epistemically limited: suspicion is not knowledge; a visible gesture is not proof of motive.

## 4. Chapter 8 technique stack

The stack is provisional and composable. No scene needs every technique.

### A. Goal-weighted sensory selection
Choose details according to what Ira is trying to do, what he fears, what he remembers, and what surprises him. A person looking for an excuse to leave notices exits and interruptions differently from someone trying to follow a conversation. Do not announce the filter; let the selection demonstrate it.

### B. Perception before verdict
Prefer the evidence that gives rise to an interpretation before stating the interpretation. Keep the evidence concrete and economical. When the character's interpretation matters, let the inference occur in a natural mental rhythm, not as a clinical explanation.

### C. Action-linked sensation
A sensation earns space when it affects posture, balance, movement, attention, comfort, confidence, or a choice. If a sleeve catches on a door handle, the catch interrupts movement; the prose should preserve that causal chain. Do not add tactile detail that has no effect simply to prove the scene is sensory.

### D. Multisensory binding
Combine channels only when they arise from the same event or shared situation: the door's movement, the sound of its latch, the draft against the hand, and the change in voices beyond it. Avoid stacking unrelated smell, taste, texture, and sound in one paragraph as a checklist. Cross-sensory detail should help readers bind information into one event.

### E. Acoustic layering (mandatory when sound is a meaningful feature)
Build an acoustic field from three to five salient layers, not a catalogue:
- **Foreground:** a nearby voice, footstep, utensil, bicycle, door, or other currently relevant source.
- **Midground:** people, business activity, movement, or conversation a little farther away.
- **Background bed:** traffic, ventilation, rain, rail noise, distant announcements, or other persistent environment.
- **Interruption/change:** a source starts, stops, gets blocked, changes direction, or becomes audible when another source ends.
- **Ira's selection:** one sound attracts or loses attention for a reason.

For each sound that matters, internally resolve:
**source → source action → path through space/material → listener position → audible result → Ira's response.**

Use only the links supported by the setting. A wall can muffle a voice; a doorway can expose it; a passing vehicle can mask speech; a hard enclosed surface can produce a more reflective sound than an open, soft, crowded space. Do not invent exact acoustic timings or technical terms in the prose unless Ira would know them. Use plain words for the result.

### F. Spatial anchoring through movement
Maintain the reader's sense of Ira's position and direction. Establish an anchor, movement, a change in relation, and the reason for a turn or stop. Use doors, corners, shopfronts, road edges, stairs, barriers, people, and light as anchors only when the scene supports them. Do not introduce every spatial feature at once. Avoid language that accidentally implies a different transport mode, elevation, room shape, or route.

### G. Body-in-space continuity
The body is not a camera floating through scenery. Preserve contact, orientation, balance, reach, obstruction, pressure, temperature, clothing movement, and effort where consequential. If something is behind Ira, its effect depends on distance, size, movement, noise, and whether he has noticed it; do not automatically claim he feels a person's presence. If a hand meets a bent or obstructed surface, preserve the actual contact and limitation. Human physiology must remain ordinary unless verified canon says otherwise.

### H. Inference gaps and bounded ambiguity
Let readers infer social meaning from behavior, context, timing, and consequences. Do not translate every look into “he was judging me” or every friendly approach into hidden manipulation. Preserve multiple explanations where Ira cannot know which is true. Later evidence can update the model. Mystery comes from a legible discrepancy, not from withholding basic spatial facts.

### I. Cognitive friction and metacognition
Show thought through operations:
- generating a possibility;
- checking it against known facts;
- noticing missing information;
- deciding what a mistake would cost;
- testing a small action;
- updating or defending a belief after feedback.

Avoid giving Ira polished academic language for insights he has not earned. His intelligence should be visible in how he handles evidence, not in how sophisticated his vocabulary sounds.

### J. Paragraphs as perceptual units
Group a sequence of mutually related perception, action, response, and interpretation into a paragraph when that makes the experience easier to reconstruct. Break when attention, spatial relation, action, speaker, or interpretation genuinely changes. Do not default to single-sentence paragraphs; do not create a wall of text. Sentence length should track the action and thought, not a fixed rhythm template.

### K. Plain lexical surface, deep underlying model
Prefer familiar words and clear syntax. The cognitive architecture can be sophisticated while the prose remains accessible. Do not force readers to Google an ordinary action or object. Use a technical term only if it is natural to Ira or necessary to the scene. The target is not simplistic prose; it is low-friction access to complex experience.

## 5. The anti-overdescription decision rule

Before retaining a detail, ask internally:

1. **Access:** could Ira perceive it from here, now?
2. **Salience:** why would it enter his attention at this moment?
3. **Function:** does it orient, affect action, alter mood, reveal social behavior, build a recurring anchor, or support inference?
4. **Causality:** does it connect to something that happens?
5. **Uniqueness:** does it add information not already supplied?
6. **Cost:** does it delay the scene or distort the reader's mental picture?

Keep the detail if it performs a clear function and the cost is justified. Compress or remove it otherwise. Do not require every detail to advance plot; atmosphere and habitation are valid functions, but they must be specific and cumulative rather than generic.

## 6. The reader-model preflight

After each paragraph or scene, ask:
- Where does the reader believe Ira is?
- What does the reader believe is happening?
- What can Ira currently sense?
- Which interpretation is Ira making, and how certain is he?
- What has changed since the previous beat?
- What will the reader expect next?
- Is any false image likely to form because a spatial or action cue is missing?
- Does the reader have to understand a technical term that could be replaced with ordinary language?

If the likely reader image differs from the intended scene, fix the causal/spatial cue. Do not blame the reader for a reasonable inference produced by ambiguous prose.

## 7. Scene engine for social encounters

Every encounter should be designed as a small social system, not an “NPC introduction.” Before prose, define:
- **Entry condition:** why Ira and the other person occupy the same situation.
- **Observable opening:** what is said or done, without assigning motive prematurely.
- **Ira's first model:** what he thinks the approach means and why.
- **Alternative model:** another plausible explanation he recognizes or overlooks.
- **Incentives:** what each person wants from the interaction, including mundane convenience, company, curiosity, status, help, amusement, or self-interest.
- **Asymmetry:** what one person knows, assumes, or notices that the other does not.
- **Micro-choice:** the smallest decision Ira must make.
- **Feedback:** the other person's response and its observable consequences.
- **Update:** what changes in Ira's model, including cases where the evidence remains inconclusive.
- **Residual:** what is still unknown when the encounter ends.

Do not give a stranger a fully articulated psychological profile before the scene earns it. The author/model may know the character's real motives for consistency; the prose should expose only what Ira can observe or infer.

## 8. Activity design: avoid fetch-quest causality

A credible activity emerges from a person's current goal meeting an independently existing opportunity or inconvenience. It should not exist only because the chapter needs a new scene.

For each activity, record:
1. Ira's current goal or discomfort.
2. The external situation already underway.
3. The plausible point of contact.
4. Why this person chooses to approach, invite, redirect, or ignore him.
5. What Ira could reasonably decline.
6. What accepting costs him (time, embarrassment, effort, uncertainty).
7. What changes if he accepts or declines.
8. How the activity reveals Kuoh without turning into a tour.
9. How it tests or complicates one of Ira's assumptions.
10. What ordinary consequence remains after the encounter.

A “mission” can be Ira's private humorous framing of a chain of mundane decisions. It must not become a game-like quest marker, arbitrary countdown, convenient stranger with a reward, or sequence of people delivering clues unless canon independently requires it.

## 9. Sensory and prose calibration tests

Use short A/B experiments before applying a technique to a full chapter.

### Test 1 — Acoustic field
Version A: names sounds.
Version B: establishes two or three distinct sources, their spatial relation, one masking/interrupting event, and Ira's selective attention.
Pass if B creates a clearer, more persistent place without making the reader work harder.

### Test 2 — Sensory restraint
Version A: describes every available sensory channel.
Version B: selects only sensations that orient, affect action, or shape interpretation.
Pass if B produces a stronger, more specific impression with fewer redundant words.

### Test 3 — Psychological inference
Version A: explains the other person's motive.
Version B: gives observable behavior, Ira's tentative inference, and a plausible alternative where needed.
Pass if the scene feels socially credible and preserves uncertainty.

### Test 4 — Spatial ambiguity
Ask a reader/model to sketch the implied arrangement or describe the movement. If the result differs from intent, revise the cues before adding detail.

### Test 5 — Voice and vocabulary
Flag terms, metaphors, sentence constructions, and aphorisms that sound more sophisticated than Ira's immediate thought. Replace only those that break voice; do not deliberately dumb down clear prose.

### Test 6 — Paragraph architecture
Check whether paragraph breaks follow changes in perceptual unit, action, attention, speaker, or inference. Remove artificial fragmentation; retain pauses that have a real dramatic function.

## 10. Known failure modes

- **Sensory inventory:** several senses are named but none affects the event.
- **Acoustic wallpaper:** every street has the same traffic hum, voices, and footsteps.
- **Omniscient perception:** Ira registers details he has no reason or opportunity to notice.
- **Interpretive overreach:** narration treats an ambiguous look or approach as proof of motive.
- **Spatial over-rendering:** too many correct details combine into the wrong overall image.
- **Spatial under-rendering:** the reader cannot tell where bodies or objects are when the action depends on it.
- **Literary overreach:** uncommon vocabulary and ornate syntax call attention to the writer rather than the experience.
- **Cognitive exposition:** Ira explains the principle instead of undergoing the reasoning.
- **Trauma monoculture:** every event is interpreted negatively and ordinary pleasure disappears.
- **Innocence caricature:** Ira's less defensive instincts are treated as stupidity.
- **Competence inflation:** one correct inference becomes proof that Ira sees through everyone.
- **Research laundering:** a paper is cited as proof of a scene-level effect it did not study.
- **Prompt outsourcing:** Gemini receives a skeletal beat list and is expected to invent psychology, motives, transitions, and causal structure. This is a production failure.

## 11. Production protocol: architecture before Gemini

The author supplies goals, constraints, lore, and corrections. The architecture model must supply the missing intermediate structure before prose generation.

Required chain:
**source/canon recovery → evidence ledger → psychological causal model → narrative function → scene activity system → beat chain → POV/attention plan → sensory/acoustic plan → prose-technique plan → Gemini master prompt → prose generation → independent audit → canon/decision update.**

Gemini's role is to maintain and execute a fully developed design at scale, not to invent the missing architecture. The handoff must include:
- locked canon and forbidden drift;
- provisional proposals clearly labelled;
- character state and causal mechanisms;
- each scene's objective, entry, interaction, choice, consequence, and exit;
- physical geography and sound sources;
- the POV's available knowledge and uncertainty;
- examples of the target prose and anti-examples;
- vocabulary/cadence constraints;
- chapter start/end state and same-night timeline;
- explicit tests and failure criteria.

## 12. Acceptance criteria

This engine is not considered calibrated merely because it reads well as a document. Chapter 8's rendered prose must pass:
- continuity and timeline audit;
- point-of-view / knowledge-boundary audit;
- spatial reconstruction test;
- acoustic-source coherence test for sound-rich scenes;
- sensory relevance and redundancy audit;
- psychological causality audit;
- social-motive plausibility audit;
- vocabulary and voice audit against Chapters 1, 2, 6, and 7;
- paragraph/cadence audit;
- canon and prohibited-content audit;
- chapter-level state transition and ending-function audit.

Status remains **PROVISIONAL** until applied to actual prose and reviewed against the source manuscripts.

## 13. Research status / next work

This is a first-pass synthesis. It is not a complete literature review of all sensory modalities, autobiographical memory, interoception, social cognition, fear learning, cyberbullying, or urban perception. Those are separate research modules to be added only when they materially change a writing decision. Do not use a broad “evolutionary biology explains 90% of everything” claim as a shortcut; identify the specific mechanism relevant to the scene and distinguish evolved capacities from learned, cultural, and individual differences.
