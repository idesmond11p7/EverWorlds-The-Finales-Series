# Chapter Six — Somewhere to Go

I woke up before the alarm.

Again.

For a few seconds I stared at the ceiling without moving.

Then I remembered where I was.

The apartment was quiet.

Morning light came through the curtains in a thin band, reaching across the floor and stopping just short of the bed. The room looked different from yesterday. Not because anything had changed. I had.

I knew where the bathroom was now.

I knew which drawer held the wallet.

I knew where I'd left the school papers.

That was a surprisingly useful improvement.

I reached for my phone.

The screen lit up.

6:41.

My alarm was set for 7:00.

I considered going back to sleep.

Then I remembered yesterday's journey.

No.

Absolutely not.

I sat up.

My hair had spread across the pillow and down my back.

I grabbed it and pulled it over one shoulder.

“Morning.”

My voice still sounded strange.

Less strange than yesterday, though.

Maybe that was the problem.

I was getting used to it.

I didn't know whether that was good or bad.

I got dressed.

The clothes I'd found yesterday were still where I'd left them. I had already decided that whoever Ira had been, he apparently had a better system for keeping clothes organised than I did.

That wasn't difficult.

I found the examination papers on the desk.

Wallet.

Money.

Identification.

Phone.

I checked everything twice.

Then a third time.

I looked at the map.

I folded it.

Unfolded it.

Checked the route again.

The station.

The transfer.

Kuoh.

The academy.

I had managed to find the place yesterday without getting completely lost.

That was something.

Today I intended to repeat the achievement.

I went into the kitchen.

There wasn't much food.

I found bread, eggs and something in the refrigerator that I wasn't entirely confident identifying.

I left that alone.

The eggs were safer.

While they cooked, I stood beside the counter and watched the morning through the window.

The neighbourhood was already moving.

A delivery van stopped at the curb.

Someone in the building opposite opened a window and shook out a piece of fabric.

A bicycle rolled past below.

The rider's tires made a soft sound over the road.

Somewhere farther away, a truck shifted gears.

The smell of cooking drifted up from another apartment.

For a few seconds I just stood there.

Yesterday, all of this had felt like evidence that I was somewhere I didn't belong.

This morning it was simply morning.

That difference was small.

It mattered anyway.

I ate quickly, washed the plate and checked the time.

7:18.

“Okay.”

I picked up my bag.

Before leaving, I stopped in the bedroom.

The apartment was still strange.

The furniture belonged to someone else.

The books belonged to someone else.

The life contained in the drawers belonged to someone else.

But I'd slept here twice.

I'd eaten here.

I'd showered here.

I'd stood in this room and cried until I couldn't anymore.

Maybe that was how places became yours.

Not because you owned them.

Because things happened there.

I locked the door behind me.

---

The street looked different in the morning.

Yesterday I'd been too busy figuring out where I was to notice much.

Now I could actually look.

The buildings were close together along the road, most of them concrete or plaster with balconies, narrow windows and air-conditioning units attached wherever someone had found enough wall space for one. Pipes ran along the sides of several buildings. Utility wires crossed above the street in messy lines.

The pavement wasn't perfectly even.

There were darker patches where yesterday's moisture had dried more slowly in the shade.

A shopkeeper was lifting a metal shutter.

The smell of frying food came from somewhere around the corner.

Two schoolchildren walked past me, talking quickly enough that I couldn't catch what they were saying.

I kept going.

The station was farther than it looked on the map.

Of course it was.

By the time I reached the main road, I was already checking the route again.

I stopped at a crossing.

Cars passed in both directions.

A bus pulled away from a stop, leaving behind a brief cloud of exhaust that disappeared almost immediately.

The traffic light changed.

People started crossing.

I crossed with them.

Five minutes later, I saw the pharmacy.

Then the station entrance.

I smiled.

“Didn't get lost.”

That felt like an achievement.

---

The station was already busy.

Not crowded enough to be uncomfortable, but busy in the way places became when large numbers of people all had somewhere to be at roughly the same time.

Shoes clicked against the floor.

A train announcement played overhead.

The words blurred together at first.

I checked the board.

Platform.

Departure.

Destination.

I matched the characters against the information I'd written down.

For once, everything made sense.

I bought a ticket and went downstairs.

The platform smelled faintly of metal, dust and whatever someone had eaten recently.

A vending machine stood near one of the pillars.

I bought a bottle of water.

Then, after looking at the remaining money in my wallet, a small packet of something that looked like it would be filling enough to count as breakfast.

I opened it while I waited.

It was sweet.

Very sweet.

I stared at the packet.

“Why is everything here trying to kill me with sugar?”

Nobody answered.

A man standing nearby glanced at me.

Then at the snack.

Then away.

I ate another piece.

It wasn't bad.

The train arrived.

Air moved along the platform before the front carriage came into view.

People shifted toward the marked areas.

The doors opened.

Everyone moved with the quiet efficiency of people who had done this hundreds of times.

I followed.

Inside, there were already plenty of passengers.

I found a place near the window and held onto the overhead strap.

The doors closed.

The train moved.

---

The first part of the journey was mostly buildings.

Rows of houses.

Apartment blocks.

Small shops squeezed between larger ones.

Parking lots.

Concrete walls.

Utility poles.

Then, gradually, things opened up.

The buildings became less tightly packed.

Trees appeared between them.

Some roads widened.

There were stretches where I could see farther than the next intersection.

I watched through the window.

People came and went at every stop.

An old man carrying a folded newspaper.

A woman with a sleeping child against her shoulder.

Three students talking over one another.

A businessman who spent almost the entire journey staring at his phone.

A little boy who kept pressing his face against the window until his mother pulled him back.

None of them knew anything about me.

That was strangely comforting.

Nobody expected me to explain where I had come from.

Nobody knew that the person standing beside them had died.

Nobody knew that the body I was wearing belonged to someone named Ira Lowe.

To them, I was just another passenger.

I looked at my reflection in the window.

Long hair.

Pale face.

Green eyes.

I looked away.

Not today.

I wasn't going to spend the whole trip thinking about myself.

Outside, the scenery kept changing.

A cluster of factories passed.

Then a broad road.

Then houses with small gardens.

Then another dense stretch of buildings.

The farther we went, the more I started recognising the rhythm of the city.

Not the exact streets.

Just the changes.

Dense.

Open.

Commercial.

Residential.

Green.

Dense again.

Yesterday I had experienced all of it as one huge unfamiliar place.

Today I could see that it had layers.

That made it easier to understand.

At the next stop, I got off.

I checked the sign.

Then the map.

Then the sign again.

Correct.

I had to transfer.

The second platform was on the opposite side of the station.

I followed the signs.

No problem.

For once, I wasn't lost.

I found a bench and sat down.

A woman beside me was eating from a paper bag.

The smell made me hungry again.

I checked the time.

8:04.

I had time.

I bought another snack from a machine.

This one wasn't nearly as sweet.

I considered that a victory.

The next train arrived.

I boarded.

---

The second train was less crowded.

I got a seat by the window.

The city outside looked different now.

There were more trees.

More low buildings.

The roads seemed wider.

Houses had more space between them.

The further we travelled, the less the buildings pressed against one another.

It wasn't rural.

Not even close.

There were still shops, cars, schools and apartment buildings.

But there was room.

Room between things.

I liked that.

I didn't know why.

Maybe because the first few days of my new life had felt like everything was pressing in at once.

A body I didn't recognise.

A name I didn't know.

A world I hadn't asked for.

A future I couldn't see.

Out here, there was space.

The train slowed.

I checked the station name.

Kuoh.

I stood.

My heart beat a little faster.

Not from fear.

Something else.

Anticipation, maybe.

I stepped off the train.

---

Kuoh was already awake.

The station opened onto a broad street with shops along both sides.

A bakery had its front windows fogged slightly from the heat inside.

A bicycle rack stood outside a convenience store.

Students moved along the pavement in groups.

Some wore uniforms.

Others wore casual clothes.

A delivery driver carried boxes into a restaurant.

A woman stood outside a florist trimming stems and dropping the cut pieces into a bucket.

The air smelled different from the neighbourhood around my apartment.

Less exhaust.

More food.

There was something green underneath it too.

Trees.

Wet leaves.

Grass.

I unfolded the map.

The academy wasn't far.

I started walking.

The route looked simple.

Which should have made me suspicious.

Ten minutes later, I reached an intersection that wasn't on the map in the way I expected.

I stopped.

The road split.

I checked the street name.

Then the map.

Then the street name again.

I knew roughly where I was.

That was the important part.

Yesterday, I would have panicked.

Today I just looked around.

A bus stop stood across the road.

There was a map of the local area posted beneath the timetable.

I walked over.

The map showed the surrounding streets in more detail than mine did.

I found the academy.

Then found my position.

I had taken the wrong branch of the road.

Not by much.

I laughed quietly.

“Okay.”

I folded my map.

“I can work with that.”

I turned around.

This time, I knew exactly where I was going.

---

The closer I got to the academy, the more students I saw.

Groups formed naturally along the sidewalks.

Some walked quickly.

Some took their time.

A few were obviously talking about the examination.

Others looked like they would rather be anywhere else.

One boy had his head buried in a book while walking and nearly collided with a utility pole.

His friend caught him by the shoulder.

“You almost died.”

“I was reading.”

“That doesn't help.”

They continued walking.

I passed them.

A bicycle went by.

Then another.

A woman stepped out of a shop carrying a paper cup and nearly bumped into a student.

“Sorry.”

“No, I'm sorry.”

They moved apart.

Normal.

All of it was normal.

I found that I liked it.

There was no great revelation in it.

No dramatic feeling.

Just the quiet relief of seeing people continue to be people.

The academy appeared beyond the trees.

I slowed.

Yesterday I had only seen it from a distance.

Today I was actually walking toward it.

The grounds were larger than they looked from outside.

Stone walls and fencing marked the perimeter in places, broken by entrances and pedestrian paths. Trees lined parts of the property, their leaves moving whenever the wind came through. The main building rose beyond them, pale walls catching the morning light while darker sections of the roof disappeared behind the upper branches.

Students moved through the gates.

Teachers stood near the entrance.

A few parents waited along the side of the road.

Someone from the administration was checking papers at a folding table.

A sign directed examination applicants toward another entrance.

I followed it.

There were already dozens of people waiting.

I joined the line.

---

The registration process was painfully ordinary.

Name.

Identification.

Application number.

Document.

Signature.

Wait.

Move forward.

Repeat.

The staff member checking my papers barely looked at me.

“Applicant number?”

I handed over the form.

He checked it.

“Ira Lowe?”

“Yes.”

“Entrance examination?”

“Yes.”

“Please wait in the designated area after registration.”

He stamped something.

“Next.”

That was it.

I walked away.

For some reason, I almost laughed.

After everything that had happened, my new existence had apparently reached the point where somebody could look at me, confirm my paperwork, and tell me to stand in a line.

There was something reassuring about that.

I found the waiting area.

Applicants were scattered across benches and along the edge of the courtyard.

Some were studying.

Some were talking.

Some were staring at their phones.

One girl sat with her eyes closed while quietly mouthing formulas to herself.

Two boys compared notes.

A parent spoke quietly with a staff member.

Somewhere behind the main building, a lawn mower started.

The engine rattled for a few seconds before settling into a steady mechanical hum.

I sat down.

The sun had climbed higher.

The shadows beneath the trees had shortened.

A breeze moved across the courtyard and lifted the ends of my hair.

I caught them before they blew into my face.

“Annoying.”

A voice nearby laughed.

I looked over.

A girl was standing a few metres away.

She was wearing the academy uniform.

Dark hair.

Glasses.

A calm expression.

She was carrying several documents against her chest and speaking quietly with another student.

I didn't know her.

There was nothing particularly strange about her.

She simply looked like someone who belonged here.

The other student said something.

She adjusted her glasses and answered.

Then she noticed me looking.

Our eyes met for a second.

I looked away.

Not because I was embarrassed.

Mostly because I didn't want to stare.

When I looked back, she had already turned away.

She continued talking.

A few seconds later, she walked toward the administrative building.

That was all.

I didn't think much of it.

I looked around again.

The courtyard had settled into a strange kind of waiting.

People weren't doing nothing.

They were all doing small things.

Checking watches.

Reading.

Talking.

Drinking water.

Adjusting bags.

Looking toward the doors.

The kind of activity that happened whenever a large group of people knew something was about to begin.

I checked my phone.

8:47.

Twelve minutes.

I leaned back.

The academy was quieter than the streets outside.

Not silent.

There were footsteps on stone.

Voices carried from the far side of the courtyard.

A door opened somewhere inside the building.

Someone laughed.

The lawn mower had stopped.

A bird landed briefly on the edge of a roof before flying away.

I looked up.

The sky was pale blue through the branches.

Sunlight reached the upper windows and left the lower ones in shade.

The glass reflected the trees.

For some reason, I found myself thinking about the apartment.

About the desk.

The kitchen.

The lock on the door.

That place still wasn't mine.

Not really.

But it was somewhere I could return to.

That was enough.

Yesterday I had been trying to understand how I had ended up here.

Today I was sitting in a school courtyard waiting to take an entrance examination.

It was ridiculous.

But it was also simple.

I had a place to sleep.

I had money.

I had a name.

I had somewhere to go tomorrow.

Maybe that was what a second life was supposed to feel like at first.

Not grand.

Not meaningful.

Just possible.

A bell rang somewhere inside the academy.

The courtyard changed almost immediately.

Students stopped talking.

Staff began moving toward the entrance.

Applicants stood.

Chairs scraped.

Bags were lifted from the ground.

People checked their papers one last time.

I stood with them.

Another bell sounded.

Louder this time.

A staff member opened the doors.

“Entrance examination applicants, please proceed inside.”

I tightened my grip on the strap of my bag.

For a second, I stood still.

Then I moved with everyone else.

Tomorrow had finally arrived.

And this time, I was here for it.
